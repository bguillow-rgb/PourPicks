A blind tasting is the most honest experiment in a bourbon collector's toolkit. Strip the label, cover the bottle, assign a code, and suddenly you're tasting with your palate instead of your assumptions. The results are sometimes flattering (you nail the distillery), often humbling (the $40 bottle outscores the $180 one), and always more useful than a labeled pour. This is how you find out what you actually like.

## Why would I bother tasting blind? Can't I just open a bottle and enjoy it?

*A question we hear often:* yes, you absolutely can, and most pours should be exactly that, no homework required. But label bias is real and well-documented. Knowing a bottle costs $300 or carries a famous distillery name changes the score you give it, even when you're trying to be objective. A 2023 academic review of sensory bias studies found that price information alone shifted perceived quality ratings by an average of 11%-15% in blind vs. labeled conditions across consumer panels. That gap is large enough to steer collecting decisions in the wrong direction.

For collectors, a blind tasting answers questions that labeled sipping can't. Does the $120 store pick genuinely outperform the $45 shelf pour from the same distillery? Does age always mean better in the expressions you favor? Is your preference for one brand style actually a preference for wheat over rye, or for lower entry proof, or simply for the bottle you've seen reviewed the most? Blind tasting separates the signal from the marketing.

## What format should I use for a blind tasting at home?

The format comes down to three choices: fully blind (you don't know what's in any glass), semi-blind (you know the list but not which glass holds which bottle), or single-blind (someone else knows, you don't). All three work. Fully blind is the gold standard for removing bias. Semi-blind is more practical for solo tastings and still strips label-in-hand influence. Single-blind works best when you want a host to manage the reveal, which is useful for small group events.

For most home collectors, the semi-blind format hits the right balance. Write a list of four bourbons you plan to taste, pour them into numbered glasses while facing away from the label or in a separate room, shuffle the order, then taste without referring back to which number is which. Score everything before looking at your key.

Here's a practical format grid:

| Format | Who Knows What's Poured | Best For | Drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fully blind | Nobody | Maximum objectivity | Requires a trusted second person to pour |
| Semi-blind | You know the list, not the order | Solo tastings | Guessing game can introduce its own bias |
| Single-blind | Host knows, tasters don't | Group events | Host must be disciplined about reveals |
| Double-blind | Nobody on tasting side knows | Research-grade rigor | Logistically complex for home use |

Keep your sample size between three and five pours. Three to four is the practical sweet spot. Any more and palate fatigue makes it much harder to pick out distinct notes. At barrel proof (110+), fatigue sets in even faster. Consider smaller pours, roughly half an ounce per sample, and keep water and unsalted crackers nearby.

## How do I actually set up the glasses without accidentally giving myself hints?

*This one comes up a lot:* the pour itself is where most home blind tastings leak information. Color is a real tell. A deeply amber, mahogany pour signals age and char level before you nose it. Proof leaves a different viscosity leg on the glass. Even a bottle shape glimpsed in your peripheral vision can trigger recognition.

Some practical counter-measures: pour in a different room, or have someone else do it. Use identical, clear glasses. Glencairn stems are ideal because they're consistent and concentrate aroma. Number the glasses with a small piece of tape at the base, not on the bowl. Cover any bottles with a cloth or bag, or store them out of sight entirely. Pour in dim (not dark) light. You want enough visibility to observe color as one data point, but not enough to read label reflections.

If you're tasting solo, the simplest method is to pour all samples at once, scramble the order, then leave the room for 60 seconds before returning. That brief interruption is usually enough to reset recognition memory.

## What should I score, and how do I do it without a fancy form?

*Readers frequently ask:* you don't need a printed rubric. You need to write something down before the reveal. That's the only non-negotiable. Memory without notes is reconstructive, and the moment you see a label, recall shifts toward the brand's reputation.

A workable four-point scorecard you can jot on an index card:

- Nose (1-10): first impression, complexity, any off-notes
- Palate (1-10): flavor development, mouthfeel, proof integration
- Finish (1-10): length, pleasant or harsh, evolution after swallowing
- Overall (1-10): gut score, would you seek this out again?

Add one or two flavor keywords per category: "caramel / dried fruit / oak" on the nose, "pepper / honey / thin" on the palate. These become far more useful after the reveal than a number alone. ## What themes produce the most useful blind tastings?

Single-variable comparisons teach the most. When you taste four wildly different bourbons blind, a wheated 8-year against a high-rye 12-year against a craft NAS, you learn about the category but not much about your own taste calibration. Change one variable at a time and the results become actionable.

Themes that consistently produce strong insight for collectors:

Age ladder: three expressions from the same distillery at different age statements, say 4-year, 8-year, and 12-year. You'll land on a concrete preference for where the wood integration peaks for your palate, which informs future buying better than any review.

Proof comparison: same brand, standard release vs. barrel-proof vs. cask-strength variant. Standard wheaters like Maker's Mark and Weller Special Reserve can feel simple at lower proof; the cask-strength versions (Larceny Barrel Proof, Maker's Mark Cask Strength) keep the wheated roundness but add weight and grip that make the style genuinely interesting. Tasting them blind tells you whether the jump in proof is worth the jump in price for your preferences.

Store pick vs. standard release: a single-barrel store pick from the same brand as the standard small-batch offering. According to secondary market data tracked by WhiskyFindr across thousands of transactions, platforms aggregate real transaction data from diverse sources to reveal what bottles actually sell for, and single-barrel picks often command 20%-40% above standard-release prices in the secondary market. A blind tasting tells you whether that premium reflects a genuine flavor difference or mostly label cachet.

Wheated vs. high-rye at matched proof: one of the more illuminating exercises for collectors building a [rotation of everyday and special-occasion bottles](/articles/how-to-build-a-bourbon-rotation). Most people find they have a strong lean one way, but many have never isolated the variable cleanly.

## How do I run the reveal, and what do I do with the results?

The reveal is the payoff. Before you flip the key, read your notes out loud if you're in a group, or at least read them to yourself and commit to your scores. The most common blind tasting mistake is retroactively softening a low score on a famous bottle once the label appears. Resist this. The pre-reveal score is the honest one.

After the reveal, compare your ranking to retail price and to any secondary market values you know. A useful exercise: sort your four bourbons by your blind score, then sort the same bottles by price. The gaps are instructive. Old Weller Antique 107, for example, is a fan favorite that has famously outperformed Pappy in blind taste tests, a result that carries real buying implications. The Pappy family's secondary premiums can reach 10-15x retail, but the blind score gap rarely matches that ratio.

Document everything. A tasting note written immediately after a blind pour and before the reveal is the cleanest data point you can generate about your own palate. Over four or five sessions, patterns emerge: you consistently rate higher-proof pours better, or you systematically prefer wheated profiles even when you'd told yourself you were a high-rye collector. That self-knowledge compounds. It makes every subsequent purchase, at retail, at a barrel pick, or at [auction](/articles/how-to-buy-bourbon-at-auction), more deliberate and less susceptible to hype. If you're building a [bourbon cellar you plan to catalog and value over time](/bourbon-inventory-app), blind tasting notes attached to specific bottles are some of the most durable reference data you'll generate.

## What are common mistakes that make blind tastings less useful?

*This one comes up a lot:* the most common error is tasting too many pours. Five is a ceiling; six or more collapses into noise above 90 proof. The second most common mistake is not cleansing the palate between samples. Unsalted crackers and still water (not sparkling, which introduces CO2 that alters perception) reset the baseline adequately. The third is inconsistent glassware: using a Glencairn for one pour and a rocks glass for another introduces a variable that skews the nose score in particular, since a tulip-shaped glass concentrates volatiles while an open-mouthed rocks glass disperses them.

One more: pouring samples of dramatically different sizes. A half-ounce is enough to score all four categories reliably. A generous one-ounce pour per sample across five bottles means you've consumed two and a half ounces of whiskey before you've written a word, which is fine for an evening of drinking but degrades the precision of a tasting exercise.

Finally, don't conflate blind tasting with [building a palate from scratch](/articles/how-to-build-a-bourbon-palate). A palate-building practice is about expanding recognition of flavor compounds over time. A blind tasting is a structured comparative experiment. Both are worth doing, but they answer different questions and require slightly different setups.
