A distillation cut point is one of those production decisions that rarely makes it onto the bottle label, but it quietly determines whether your pour is rich and oily or clean and grain-forward. If you've ever wondered why two bourbons from the same mash bill taste so different, or what a master distiller is actually *doing* during distillation, this is the answer worth understanding.

## Okay, what actually is a cut point, and why should I care as a collector?

Every bourbon distillation run produces three distinct fractions: heads, hearts, and tails. The spirit doesn't arrive as one uniform liquid. What comes off the still changes character continuously over time, and cut points are simply the moments when the distillery switches from collecting one fraction to the next. Each fraction carries different compounds, different ABV, different aromas, and different flavor impacts. The distiller's job is to decide what to keep and what to discard.

For collectors, this matters because the cut point is set before a single drop of new make spirit enters a barrel. No amount of aging reverses a cut made too early or too late. The flavor architecture of every bottle you own was partially determined in those few minutes of live decision-making on the production floor.

## Who actually makes this call, and how do they make it?

*A question we hear often:* Is cutting to hearts and tails automated, or is it genuinely a human judgment call?

Overwhelmingly human, and that's not romanticism, it's production reality. Setting the cut points is one of the most consequential responsibilities of the master distiller, based on experience, observation of the distillate, and direct evaluation of its aroma and alcohol strength.

Most distillers track parameters like time elapsed since distillation began, volume collected, distillate ABV, and flow rate. Those numbers inform the decision. But almost all distillers make the final call by taste and smell.

The precise point at which a cut is made shapes the character of a spirit more than almost any other single variable, which is exactly why master distillers at major Kentucky houses spend years developing their sensory vocabulary before they ever control a cut. When you're tasting a distillery-exclusive or single-barrel expression, you're tasting that person's judgment call on a specific day.

## What happens chemically during each cut?

*This one comes up a lot:* Collectors who've studied heads, hearts, and tails often want to know what's actually in each fraction, not just the names.

The first fraction to emerge is the heads, also called foreshots. The most volatile compounds boil off at lower temperatures as the still heats up. These are undesirable: they either taste bad, pose health risks, or both, so they get separated and discarded.

Next comes the hearts, the fraction that actually becomes bourbon. The first cut point arrives when the master distiller judges that the heads have condensed out and the remaining flow is worth keeping. That's when the distillate gets redirected into a second container for collection. Everything in this fraction will eventually go into a cask.

The second cut comes later. As temperatures climb and ethanol depletes, the remaining, less-volatile compounds start coming over. These are the tails, or feints, carrying heavy fusel oils, fatty acids, and residual water. Like the heads, they're separated out rather than kept.

## Wide cut vs. narrow cut, what does each one taste like in the glass?

| Cut Style | Heart Width | Flavor Profile | Texture | Common in |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Narrow cut | Tight; excludes outer fractions | Clean, grain-forward, lighter | Silky, less oily | Premium craft expressions, lighter wheaters |
| Standard cut | Industry middle ground | Balanced sweetness + complexity | Medium-bodied | Most flagship bourbons |
| Wide cut | Broad; captures more heads/tails boundary | Rich, fruity, oily, more intense | Full, often viscous | High-rye bourbons, some pot still expressions |

A wider heart cut allows more heavy aromatic compounds into the distillate, producing a richer, more full-bodied whiskey. A narrower cut produces something cleaner and lighter. Neither is objectively better. It comes down to the house style a distillery is building toward.

One thing worth knowing: bourbon producers often retain some heads deliberately, because certain esters that develop in those compounds continue maturing as the spirit ages in barrel. What looks like a "wider" cut on paper can be a calculated flavor investment in what the barrel will become.

## Does this work differently on a column still vs. a pot still?

*Readers frequently ask:* Since virtually all bourbon is made on column stills, does the cut-point concept even translate?

Yes, but the mechanics differ significantly. In batch pot still distillation, the distiller physically redirects the flow of spirit between vessels at two discrete moments. It's a switch you flip. The heads come first, you discard them, then you collect the hearts, then you cut off when the tails start coming through.

Column stills, the workhorse of bourbon production, run continuously rather than in batches. Rather than flipping a valve, the distiller adjusts draw-off points and column architecture. A taller column with more plates can effectively "cut" the heads by pulling distillate from a higher point in the column instead of lower down where the heart comes out. The underlying question is the same (which compounds do we keep?) but it's answered through engineering decisions about column height, plate count, and draw-off location rather than a manual redirect. Some larger distilleries run their columns without stopping for days at a time, meaning the cut is baked into the architecture of the still itself.

This is one reason pot still bourbon expressions (a small but growing category) can taste so different from their column-still counterparts even when the mash bill is identical. The cut is made differently and at a different granularity. ## Can a distillery's cut point philosophy change over time?

Yes, and this is relevant to collectors building vertical tastings or tracking older vintage bottles. A change in master distiller often correlates with a subtle shift in cut philosophy, even when the mash bill and aging spec stay the same. This is why long-time collectors report that certain expressions "drank differently" in earlier years. That's not imagined nostalgia. It's genuine production drift.

Distilleries rarely publish their cut-point parameters. Most won't even reveal distillation proof, so cut points are closely guarded. That opacity is part of what makes distillery-specific house character so durable as intellectual property. When you do a [vertical tasting](/articles/what-is-a-bourbon-vertical-tasting) across five years of the same expression, you may actually be tasting, among other variables, the accumulated cut decisions of the same distiller or their successor.

## How do cut points show up in what I'm actually tasting at the pour?

*This one comes up a lot:* How do you detect cut-point influence in a finished bourbon, especially one that's been aged for years?

The clearest indicators survive aging surprisingly well. A bourbon from a wider cut typically shows more fusel-adjacent character: intense stone fruit, floral ester notes, or a heavy, oily mouthfeel. Tails are rich in fusel oils, and past a certain threshold they turn acrid and bitter. When a wide cut is managed skillfully, though, those heavier compounds integrate over oak time, contributing the viscous texture and layered fruit that high-proof collectors prize.

A narrow-cut bourbon tends toward grain presence, clean sweetness, and a lighter mid-palate. If you're tasting a barrel-proof expression and notice it feels surprisingly airy or clean despite the high proof, that's often a narrow-cut house style at work. These distinctions show up most clearly in unfiltered, barrel-proof releases where nothing has been blended away.
