A DSP number is one of the most useful and most overlooked codes hiding in plain sight on your bourbon bottles. For collectors building a cellar, hunting dusties, or verifying the provenance of a sourced release, understanding this small string of characters can tell you something no marketing copy ever will: exactly where the whiskey inside was actually made.

## So what exactly is a DSP number, and why should I care?

There is genuine confusion about DSP numbers and what they mean, and the system itself has evolved considerably over the roughly 150 years since the federal government first created it for tax purposes.

DSP stands for "Distilled Spirits Plant," and the code tells you where a particular bottle was distilled. On a Kentucky bourbon you'll see it formatted as DSP-KY-[number]. Other states use their own two-letter abbreviation in the middle.

For everyday drinking, the number is background noise. For collectors it's a provenance fingerprint. When a brand changes hands, sources from a new distillery, or releases a vintage expression, the DSP on the label is often the only reliable way to confirm actual production origin, especially on bottles where the brand name stayed the same but the liquid inside did not.

## How did the DSP system get started?

*A question we hear often:* why does a number that looks like a tax code matter so much to bourbon nerds?

When Lincoln brought back the excise tax on distilled spirits to fund the Civil War, the government also created bonded warehouses, because taxing spirits as they came off the still was seen as unfair to distillers who aged their products. Each production facility had to be registered and numbered so the government could track what was being made and where.

Kentucky was broken into districts and assigned Registered Distillery (RD) numbers during the Civil War era, but that system proved confusing as names were shared across county lines. In 1956, the Internal Revenue Service redesignated the permits as DSP numbers. The format stuck, and today every licensed distilling operation in the United States carries one.

Each distilling still was registered independently. The government was tracking production of spirits, not land, so if a distillery expanded by adding a new still, that still required its own separate registration. Multiple DSP numbers did not necessarily indicate different properties or even different buildings.

## Where do I actually find the DSP number on my bottles?

*This one comes up a lot:* collectors know the number exists but aren't sure where to look.

On Bottled-in-Bond expressions, the DSP number is legally required to appear on the label. It's part of what makes a BiB verifiable. Look near the bottled-by or distilled-by statement, usually in small type on the back label or near the tax strip on vintage bottles.

On standard (non-bonded) bottles, it's optional and placement varies. Check the back label near the producer's address, the capsule, or the paper strip stamp on older bottles. Some brands print it prominently as a mark of heritage; others omit it entirely.

On vintage and dusty finds, the DSP is often your best friend. Occasionally the last two digits of a bottle's bottling year are embossed in the bottom glass. By examining the barcode's first five digits, you can often identify who actually bottled a given whiskey, something worth knowing when bourbon brands changed hands so frequently while labels often remained nearly identical.

## Does a low DSP number mean the distillery is old and prestigious?

*Readers frequently ask* this after noticing brands market their low DSP numbers as a badge of heritage.

The honest answer: not reliably. As bourbon attorney and author Brian Haara has noted, distillers seem to believe many consumers think a low DSP number signifies the age of the distillery, and those who promote their low numbers probably want consumers to think exactly that. But it isn't always the case.

DSP numbers could be transferred with a distillery sale if ongoing operations continued, cancelled if not in use, or even reused if the registered distillery had formally closed. A brand operating today with a single-digit or low two-digit DSP may have simply inherited that number when they purchased an old facility.

Kentucky Peerless, revived in 2014, carries DSP-50. Old Pogue, which opened in 1876, holds DSP-3 but changed hands before returning to the original family. Castle & Key, formerly Old Taylor, holds DSP-18 even though the modern brand was founded in 2014.

Newer operations like Angel's Envy and Bulleit have DSP numbers in the five-figure range. That tells you roughly when they first registered, nothing more.

## What are the most important DSP numbers for bourbon collectors to know?

Here's a quick-reference table of the Kentucky DSP numbers that come up most often in collecting circles, dusty hunting, and vintage bottle research:

| DSP Number | Distillery / Facility | Why Collectors Care |
|---|---|---|
| DSP-KY-1 | Heaven Hill (Bernheim) | One of the oldest continuously operating registered numbers in KY |
| DSP-KY-8 | Four Roses, Lawrenceburg | Confirms authentic Four Roses distillate across eras |
| DSP-KY-14 | Old Grand-Dad (National Distillers era) | Pre-1987 bottles with this DSP are sought-after dusties |
| DSP-KY-16 | Stitzel-Weller | Finding a bottle with that code is considered a genuine find. Home of original Pappy distillate |
| DSP-KY-354 | Buffalo Trace (Ancient Age/George T. Stagg) | Confirms Buffalo Trace-distilled sourced bourbons |
| DSP-KY-19 | Old Taylor (National Distillers era) | ND Old Taylor was distilled at the "Castle" distillery under DSP-KY-19 |

Outside Kentucky, the same format applies: DSP-IN for Indiana, DSP-TN for Tennessee, and so on. Indiana's MGP distillery, the source behind many well-known sourced bourbons, carries its own DSP number that shows up on a surprising number of mainstream labels if you know to look.

## How do DSP numbers help when I'm hunting sourced or NDP bottles?

This is where DSP research earns its keep. Many non-distiller producers (NDPs) purchase whiskey from large distilleries and bottle it under their own brand. The label may say "Bottled by" rather than "Distilled by," a subtle but legally meaningful difference explained in detail in our guide to [what is sourced bourbon](https://pourpicks.app/articles/what-is-sourced-bourbon).

When both a distilled-by and bottled-by statement appear and the DSP numbers differ between them, that's your clearest signal that the whiskey was made somewhere other than the bottler's facility. Cross-referencing the distiller's DSP against known databases helps you verify which actual production facility the spirit came from, whether Buffalo Trace, MGP, Heaven Hill, or elsewhere.

Some of the most sought-after vintage bourbons came from the National Distillers company, distilled from the 1960s through the 1990s. While not always recognized today as premium pours, Old Taylor and Old Grand-Dad bourbons from the National Distillers era can be remarkable whiskeys. The two easiest ways to identify ND-distilled bourbons are by the DSP number (for bonded whiskeys) and by the UPC code (for non-bonded).

National Distillers' Old Grand-Dad was made at the Old Grand-Dad distillery under DSP-KY-14. Some bottles were actually distilled at Old Taylor but brought to Old Grand-Dad for bottling. Both labels used ND UPC codes beginning with 86259. Knowing both the DSP and the UPC prefix together gives you a much more complete picture of what's in the bottle than the label text alone.

## Does the DSP number matter for modern releases, or just vintage bottles?

Both, though the use cases differ. For modern bottles, the DSP is most useful when:

- Verifying sourced releases: if the DSP on the label points to Buffalo Trace or MGP, you know where the distillate came from regardless of what the brand's story implies.
- Comparing releases across years: if a brand's DSP changes between the 2022 and 2025 releases of the same expression, the liquid almost certainly changed too. For collectors building vertical tastings (see our [vertical tasting guide](https://pourpicks.app/articles/what-is-a-bourbon-vertical-tasting)), this is a detail worth logging.
- Reading Bottled-in-Bond labels accurately: since BiB regulations require the DSP to appear, it's always verifiable. Our guide to [what is Bottled-in-Bond](https://pourpicks.app/articles/what-is-bottled-in-bond-bourbon) covers the full legal standard those labels must meet.

For vintage and dusty hunting, the DSP is often the single most important authentication tool you have, more reliable than the brand name, which may have been sold and relicensed multiple times.

If you use Pour Picks to catalog your collection, logging the DSP number alongside each bottle's entry is a good habit, especially for bottles you're holding long-term. It becomes part of the provenance record if you ever sell, insure, or simply want to remember exactly what era and facility a pour came from.

## FAQs

**What does DSP stand for on a bourbon bottle?**
DSP stands for Distilled Spirits Plant. It is a federally assigned permit number that identifies the specific facility where the spirit was produced. On bourbon labels you'll see it formatted as DSP-KY-[number] for Kentucky distilleries, or DSP-[STATE]-[number] for other states.

**Where do I find the DSP number on a bourbon bottle?**
On Bottled-in-Bond bourbons it is required by law and typically appears on the front or back label near the bottling statement. On non-bonded bottles it may appear in small print near the producer address or on the strip stamp. Vintage bottles often show it prominently on the tax strip.

**Does a low DSP number mean a distillery is old or better quality?**
Not necessarily. Low DSP numbers were assigned in order of registration, but distilleries change hands and permits transfer with sales. Kentucky Peerless, revived in 2014, holds DSP-50, while some modern craft operations inherited very low numbers from prior owners. A low number reflects registration history, not current quality.

**What are some iconic DSP numbers collectors should know?**
DSP-KY-16 is Stitzel-Weller, home of the original Pappy Van Winkle distillate. DSP-KY-1 is Heaven Hill's Bernheim distillery. DSP-KY-8 is Four Roses in Lawrenceburg. DSP-KY-14 was Old Grand-Dad under National Distillers. DSP-KY-354 is Buffalo Trace. Knowing these helps you verify the origin of vintage and Bottled-in-Bond finds.

**Can the same bourbon brand have different DSP numbers across releases?**
Yes. If a brand sources from multiple distilleries, or if distillery ownership changes, the DSP on the label can change between releases. This is one reason collectors cross-reference DSP numbers when building verticals -- different DSP numbers on the same brand label can indicate meaningfully different liquid inside.
