A bourbon dump date is one of those collector terms that sounds self-explanatory until you actually try to explain it to someone, and then you realize there's quite a bit packed into two words. Here's what it means, why enthusiasts build hunts around it, and how to use the information once you have a bottle in hand.

## "Okay, but what actually IS a dump date -- like, what is being dumped?"

*A question we hear often:* the word "dump" sounds almost industrial, which is fitting. It describes a very physical act.

The dump date is the day the bourbon leaves the barrel. Distilleries write this day on the label because it is the day the bourbon stops barrel aging. Workers literally tip, or "dump," the barrel so its contents flow out into a receiving tank, ending the wood-contact maturation that's been going on for years. Once the barrel is dumped (which could be one or two days before bottling), the bourbon is filtered, proofed, and held until it's time to bottle.

So the dump date is not the bottling date. It's the moment the clock stops on aging, and for a single-barrel expression it's the closest thing to a "born on" date for that specific liquid.

---

## Why does this matter specifically for single-barrel bourbon?

Because a single-barrel bourbon, by definition, comes from one individual barrel. Each bottling batch is produced from the contents of only one particular aging barrel, not mixed with whiskey from any other barrels. That means every label detail, barrel number, rickhouse location, and dump date, is unique to that specific vessel. No two bottles share all three identifiers.

This is fundamentally different from a small-batch or blended expression, where hundreds of barrels are married together and individual barrel provenance disappears. With a single barrel, the dump date becomes a kind of fingerprint.

---

## Which bourbons actually print the dump date on the label?

*This one comes up a lot:* collectors sometimes assume all single-barrel releases carry dump dates, but that's not the case.

Blanton's Single Barrel Bourbon changed American whiskey in 1984 when it became the first commercially marketed single-barrel bourbon, each bottle drawn from one individual barrel rather than a blended batch, stamped with the barrel number and rick location where it aged. And because each bottle came from a single barrel, it could feature the literal date it was dumped. For roughly the last 28 years, every single bottle of Blanton's has had an exact date hand-scrawled on the first line of the label by one of the dozen or so bottling hall employees working that day.

Beyond Blanton's, many store picks and private barrel selections include dump dates on neck tags or back labels, but that's at the retailer's or distillery's discretion, not a federal labeling requirement. Blanton's remains the flagship case study because it's both widely collected and consistently transparent about the date.

For the export market, Blanton's goes even further. Blanton's Straight From The Barrel is the cask-strength expression produced for the Japanese export market, released at barrel proof, typically between 125 and 132 proof. Each bottle is individually numbered from its barrel and dated with the fill date, dump date, and bottling date. That trifecta of dates is unusual in the industry and makes SFTB bottles especially prized by collectors tracking provenance.

## What else does a Blanton's label tell you?

Here's a quick breakdown of what's hand-written on a standard Blanton's Original Single Barrel label:

| Label Field | What It Means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Dump date | Day the barrel was emptied; aging ends | 03-23-21 |
| Barrel number | Sequential ID for that specific barrel | Barrel No. 171 |
| Rick number | Shelf position within Warehouse H | Rick No. 3 |
| Warehouse | Always Warehouse H for Blanton's Original | H |
| Proof | Bottling strength (hand-written, always 93) | 93 |

Blanton's Single Barrel Bourbon is typically aged 6 to 8 years in Warehouse H at Buffalo Trace, the only metal-clad warehouse on the property, commissioned for construction by one of the distillery's early leaders, Albert B. Blanton. Being metal, the warehouse transfers heat faster than brick warehouses, which allows for more rapid aging.

Worth noting: the rick number matters to some collectors because higher floors in a metal warehouse experience more heat cycling, which can affect flavor concentration and extraction. For a deeper look at how warehouse position shapes a pour, see our article on [how rickhouse floor position affects bourbon flavor](/articles/how-rickhouse-floor-position-affects-bourbon-flavor).

## Why do collectors hunt specific dump dates -- isn't any bottle just as good?

*Readers frequently ask:* "Is this just sentimentality, or is there actually a flavor argument?"

Mostly sentimentality, and that's a perfectly valid reason to collect. The most common driver is personal significance: a collector wants a bottle dumped on the day their child was born, a wedding anniversary, a milestone birthday. Because bourbon is something you open and share, owning a bottle from a personally meaningful date turns a pour into a story.

Hunting for these bottles isn't like hunting for other ultra-rare whiskeys like Pappy, which has more to do with your willingness and ability to spend money. Bottles with a singular date on them, even just a year and a month, are magnitudes harder to track down. That's the other appeal: the hunt itself. Unlike chasing allocated releases that drop on a known schedule, dump-date hunting requires patience, relationships with store employees, and a bit of luck.

Is there a flavor difference between dump dates? In theory, two barrels dumped a week apart from the same rickhouse at similar rick positions and ages should be quite similar. The more meaningful flavor variable is rick position and age, not the specific calendar date. Within a single-barrel expression, though, no two barrels are ever identical, so some dump dates do earn reputations in collector communities for being particularly outstanding pours. That's largely anecdotal, but it's not nothing.

## How do collectors actually find a specific dump date?

There are numerous websites, social media groups, and apps to help locate a specific dump date. Almost daily, Reddit users post to r/bourbon looking for specific ones. The granddaddy of tracking tools is bourbondumpdate.com, which launched in 2019.

The practical playbook most collectors use:

1. Build retailer relationships. Employees who handle incoming Blanton's shipments can tell you the dump dates on bottles sitting in the stockroom before they hit the shelf. This is the single highest-leverage move.
2. Join community forums and Facebook groups. Collectors post available dump dates; others request specific ones. Trades happen regularly. See our guide to [trading bourbon bottles](/articles/how-to-trade-bourbon-bottles) if you're new to that side of the hobby.
3. Use dedicated tracking sites. Bourbondumpdate.com aggregates self-reported sightings by date and retailer location, useful for narrowing a geographic search.
4. Check the secondary market. Specific dump dates command premiums on auction platforms, particularly for milestone years. Our article on [buying bourbon at auction](/articles/how-to-buy-bourbon-at-auction) covers how to navigate that process safely.

One more piece of useful intel: the distillery generally dumps barrels Monday through Friday. That means weekend and holiday dates are essentially impossible to find, which matters when you're hunting a specific day of the week.

## Does the dump date affect how I should catalog the bottle in my collection?

It absolutely should, and this is where systematic record-keeping pays off. If you're building a collection that includes single-barrel releases, logging the dump date alongside barrel number and rick position gives you a meaningful provenance record that goes well beyond "Blanton's, 93 proof."

If you're currently tracking your bottles in a spreadsheet, you've probably already noticed that keeping dump dates, barrel numbers, and rick info organized gets unwieldy fast. ## Is hunting dump dates worth the effort if I'm not a hardcore Blanton's collector?

*This one comes up a lot:* collectors who aren't specifically into Blanton's sometimes wonder if the dump-date concept applies to anything they actually own.

The short answer: yes, the principle extends widely, even if the label transparency doesn't. Many private barrel and store-pick releases from distilleries like Four Roses, Wild Turkey, Heaven Hill, and others include dump dates on neck tags or back labels as part of the barrel selection record. When you're doing a [bourbon barrel pick](/articles/how-to-do-a-bourbon-barrel-pick) or buying a [store pick](/articles/what-is-a-bourbon-store-pick), the dump date is part of what makes that bottle a distinct, one-time release.

For general collectors, the takeaway is simpler: any time you see a dump date on a label, note it. It's the clearest timestamp available for when that specific whiskey stopped aging, and in a hobby built around provenance and story, that timestamp is worth keeping.
