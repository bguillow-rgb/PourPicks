Starting a bourbon tasting club is one of the more rewarding things a collector can do with their cellar. You get honest feedback on bottles you've been hoarding, your palate sharpens faster than solo sipping ever will, and you build the kind of friendships that come with shared pours. This guide covers how to recruit the right group, which formats actually work, how to run a blind session without it turning into chaos, and how to keep momentum across many months of meetings.

## "Do I actually need a formal club, or can I just invite friends over?"

*A question we hear often:* and the honest answer is that an informal gathering is exactly where most great tasting clubs start. The difference between a casual hangout and a club that's still meeting two years later is a little shared structure: an agreed format, a rotation, and a scoring sheet. Without those three things, sessions drift toward the same familiar bottles and the same comfortable opinions. With them, every meeting teaches you something you didn't know before.

Don't overcomplicate the launch, though. A bourbon tasting club can be as simple as six people at someone's house once a month, opening three bottles and comparing notes. Or it can be as elaborate as a formal society that organizes distillery visits and private barrel picks. Start small. Add structure as the group finds its rhythm.

## "How many people should I invite, and who?"

Keeping the guest list tight makes discussion easier. Cap it at around ten people and you only need one bottle of each bourbon. In practice, six to eight members is the sweet spot for collector-focused groups. Fewer than five gives you thin score data and makes splitting bottle costs awkward; more than ten and conversations fragment.

Mix experience levels on purpose. Scout participants from your social circles, work colleagues, or bourbon-focused online forums. A blend of novices and people who've been drinking seriously for years creates a room where both learning and debate can happen. The novices ask the questions veterans stopped asking; the veterans fill in context that keeps things from going in circles.

One practical rule: every member should own at least a handful of bottles at home. That gives the group a deep enough combined cellar to theme sessions around obscure categories, wheated bourbons, single barrel picks from one distillery, bottles within a specific age range, without anyone scrambling to the nearest liquor store at the last minute.

## "Blind or open-label -- which format is actually better?"

*This one comes up a lot.* Both have a legitimate place in a well-run club, and the answer depends on what you want out of a given session.

Blind tastings are the most valuable format for building your palate and cutting through label bias. A blind tasting reveals preconceptions about bourbon you didn't know you held. In one documented blind session, two separate pours that members judged differently on origin and flavor turned out to be identical bottles, a reminder that our brains fill in a lot of what we think we're perceiving. Proof matters here too: tasting a barrel-proof bourbon before an 80-proof expression will make the lower-proof bottle taste like water. Keep all bottles within a 10-proof-point range. If the lightest is 90 proof, the heaviest should be around 100.

Open-label tastings shine when context adds value: a distillery rep joins, a rare bottle deserves a story, or you're doing a deliberate vertical across multiple releases of the same expression. Many experienced clubs alternate by default, blind sessions one month for honest scoring, open sessions the next when the backstory enriches the pour.

| Format | Best For | Watch Out For |
|---|---|---|
| Fully blind | Honest palate calibration, breaking label bias | Need a neutral third party to pour and mask bottles |
| Semi-blind (category revealed) | Comparing within a style -- e.g., "all wheated" | Members may still guess brands and anchor scores |
| Open-label | Education, distillery guests, rare bottle context | Confirmation bias inflates scores for famous names |
| Themed vertical | Tracking one brand across years or batches | Requires owning or sourcing multiple specific releases |

## "What's the right pour size so nobody ends up over-served?"

Plan on about 0.5 oz per glass per bourbon. A standard 750 ml bottle yields roughly 25 half-ounce tasting pours, enough to cover six to eight members across three samples with room to revisit. At a six-bottle session with eight members, that's 3 oz of total liquid per person over the course of an evening, consumed slowly over two or more hours, alongside water and palate cleansers.

Set up water and light snacks before guests arrive. Crackers, nuts, and mild cheese work well without coating the palate or introducing strong competing flavors. Some clubs keep coffee beans on the table for nasal cleansing between pours; distilled water droppers and glasses of water to sip between samples are standard at well-organized tastings.

Don't frame an evening around consumption volume. The goal is appreciation, the same principle that drives serious wine and spirits education, not getting through as many pours as possible.

## "How do I structure a session so it doesn't fall apart mid-evening?"

*Readers frequently ask* about the logistics of actually running a session, and this is where most casual groups stall. The answer is simple: do the prep work before anyone arrives.

Before guests arrive, pour all samples into numbered or lettered glasses. For blind sessions, hide the bottles completely before anyone walks in. Cover each glass as well; whiskey begins to oxidize shortly after it's poured, and covering slows that. Place tasting sheets, pens, and water at each seat. Decide your pour order in advance: lightest to strongest, so bigger bourbons don't flatten everything that came before them. Starting with a gentle, balanced bourbon and finishing with a barrel-proof expression is the right direction. Doing it the other way around will wreck your palate for the first half of the flight.

During the session, give members 3-5 minutes of silent evaluation per pour before discussion opens. Have a designated host who keeps the pacing and writes down group scores before the reveal. Do the reveal after everyone has committed their scores in writing. That's the moment that generates the best conversation.

For scoring, keep it simple: Nose (1-5), Palate (1-5), Finish (1-5), Overall (1-10). A consistent rubric means your scores from month six are actually comparable to your scores from month one.

## "How do we decide what theme to taste each month?"

Bourbons of different ages work well, as does focusing on a particular region. After scoring, reveal a one-page dossier detailing each bourbon's background, mash bill, distillery, age, proof, so the context lands after honest impressions are already on paper.

Theme ideas that consistently generate strong discussion in collector circles:

- Proof bracket: all bottles 100-110 proof, any distillery
- One distillery, multiple expressions: all Four Roses labels from yellow to SBLE, for example
- Wheated vs. high-rye: side-by-side contrast of the two major mash bill camps
- Age statement showdown: four, eight, twelve, and eighteen-year expressions of the same or similar mash bills
- Store picks vs. core: a distillery's standard single barrel next to two retailer-selected private barrels from the same distillery
- Price-blind value test: bottles ranging from $30 to $130 scored without price context, to see where perceived value actually lands

That last one is particularly humbling. Blind tastings have shown that a sub-$30 shelf bottle can hold its own against a seldom-seen $100 bottle when label and price are removed from the equation.

## "How do we keep the club going month after month?"

*A question we hear often* from groups that launched strong but faded by month four. The clubs that last share a few structural traits.

Rotate hosting. One person burning all the organizational energy is the fastest route to burnout. Assign a host and curator role for each meeting. That person picks the theme, sources or coordinates the bottles, and runs the session. Everyone else just shows up.

Keep a shared archive. A spreadsheet or shared doc logging every session's theme, bottles, scores, and tasting notes becomes invaluable by year two. It lets you revisit a bottle from 18 months ago, run a "greatest hits" session, or track how a particular release has changed across batch years. Pour Picks works well for the individual journal side of this: every member logs their private notes bottle by bottle, and the group compares after the blind reveal rather than before, so no one anchors anyone else's scoring.

Plan a milestone session. A "best of the year" session in December, where you revisit the top-scored pours from the previous 11 months, is the kind of event that cements a club's identity and keeps renewal interest high. Comparing different bottlings over time and building meaningful verticals adds depth and makes individual releases easier to contextualize year after year.

## "Do we need any formal rules about sourcing bottles?"

You need one rule above all others: agree on a per-bottle cost ceiling before the first session. Clubs fracture when one member shows up with a $250 bottle expecting reciprocal contributions at that level while others are working with a $50 ceiling. Set a range ($40-$80 is workable for most groups), rotate who sources what, and revisit the ceiling once a year.

Beyond budget, establish a few basic norms: arrive on time because poured glasses oxidize, score before you speak, and no revealing guesses out loud during the blind phase. Simple etiquette prevents the one vocal member from anchoring everyone else's notes before they've had a chance to form their own.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many people is ideal for a bourbon tasting club?**
Five to ten members is the sweet spot. With fewer than five you lose meaningful score variation; above ten you need multiple bottles of each expression and conversation fragments. Six or seven lets one 750 ml bottle cover everyone at a 0.5 oz pour with margin to revisit.

**Should a bourbon tasting club be blind or open-label?**
Both formats have real value. Blind tastings strip out label bias and surface what you actually prefer; open tastings let context and distillery backstory enrich the discussion. Many clubs alternate: blind sessions for honest scoring, open sessions when a guest or rare bottle is the centerpiece.

**How often should a bourbon tasting club meet?**
Monthly is the most sustainable cadence. It gives members time to source bottles, rotate hosting without burnout, and keeps enthusiasm high. Quarterly works for clubs that invest in larger, more elaborate themed sessions.

**How do you keep proof consistent in a blind tasting?**
Build your flight within a 10-proof-point window. A high-proof barrel-strength pour tasted before an 80-proof expression will flatten the lighter bottle's subtleties and skew everyone's scores.

**How do you track tasting notes across multiple sessions?**
Standardize your scoring sheet from day one, nose, palate, finish, overall on a consistent scale, and keep a shared log. Pour Picks lets every member journal individual pours privately; you compare notes after the reveal so no one's comments anchor the group's scoring before it happens.
