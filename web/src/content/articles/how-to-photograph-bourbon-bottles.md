Good bourbon photography is not about art direction. It's about documentation. Whether you're building a cellar catalog, preparing an insurance inventory, listing a bottle on the secondary market, or just capturing a rare find before you crack it open, a sharp, honest photograph is the most useful record you can make. This guide is written for collectors, not commercial photographers, and every tip here works with equipment you already own.

## Why do collectors even need to photograph their bottles?

*A question we hear often:* "I know what I have, why bother taking photos?"

The honest answer: you know what you have *right now*. Three years from now, when you're pulling a bottle for a tasting, trying to remember what year you bought a particular barrel pick, filing an insurance claim after a flooded basement, or selling something on the secondary market, a photograph does the remembering for you.

Collectors should capture images from all angles, because there may be pertinent information on the rear label. For serious collectors, the seal is often the most significant detail, along with the fill level.

Documentation photography and aesthetic photography are two different goals. This guide focuses on the first: clear, accurate, repeatable shots that serve your collection over the long haul. Apps like Pour Picks let you attach photos directly to each bottle entry in your cellar catalog, so every image is searchable and tied to your tasting notes and valuations, not buried in your camera roll.

## What gear do I actually need, do I have to buy anything?

Almost certainly no. More people than ever are sharing whisky experiences through photography, whether using dedicated cameras for serious bottle shots or sharing phone snaps of the pour of the day. A recent flagship smartphone produces images that are more than adequate for cellar records and secondary-market listings.

That said, two low-cost additions make a measurable difference:

| Tool | Budget Option | Why It Helps |
|---|---|---|
| Tripod or phone grip | $15-30 mini tripod | Eliminates camera shake; critical in low light |
| Diffusion material | Parchment paper from your kitchen | Softens harsh lamps or window light |
| Backdrop | White foam board or a pillowcase | Neutral background keeps label colors true |
| Light tent / box | $25-40 collapsible cube | All-in-one diffusion for tabletop shots |
| Dedicated camera | Your existing phone | Sufficient for 95% of collector use cases |

If you own a DSLR or mirrorless camera, it shines in low-light cellars where phones still struggle. But don't wait to buy gear. Start with what you have and improve incrementally.

## How do I handle the hardest part, glare on glass?

*This one comes up a lot.* Glass bourbon bottles are genuinely tricky because they simultaneously reflect the room around them and transmit light through the liquid inside. Get the lighting wrong and you end up with a white hotspot over the label, or a liquid that photographs as opaque brown sludge.

The main challenge in whisky photography is reflection from the glass. Soft lighting is the best fix, though it brings its own considerations. The practical approach for collectors without studio gear: shiny surfaces need soft light. On a budget, tape baking or parchment paper over your flash or lamp, and fold it more times for even softer results.

Three reliable setups for collectors, ranked by ease:

1. North-facing window on an overcast day. Clouds act as a giant natural diffuser. Position the bottle 2-3 feet from the glass, label facing the window. This is the single most reliable free setup.
2. Light tent on a kitchen counter. A $30 collapsible cube surrounds the bottle with diffused light from every angle. Diffuse lighting is the key here, and the closer and larger the light source relative to the bottle, the softer the result.
3. Backlight glow trick. Whiskey liquid tends to read as flat brown in photos. Placing a small LED panel or even a phone screen behind and below the bottle illuminates the liquid from within and gives it visible depth.

One thing to avoid without exception: direct camera flash aimed straight at the label. Flash produces severe shadows and kills label clarity.

## What angles should every bottle entry include?

For pure documentation (the kind that supports an insurance claim or a secondary listing) you need at minimum four shots. For a complete cellar record, six is the standard.

The essential four:
- Full front label: straight on, filling the frame, all text sharp
- Full back label: same approach; batch codes, barrel numbers, and distillery disclaimers live here
- Neck and capsule: top-down view showing the seal condition, wax color if applicable, or revenue strip integrity
- Fill level from the side: shoot with the lens at shoulder height so the liquid line is accurate

Two extras worth adding:
- Lot/batch code: often embossed or stamped on the bottle bottom or shoulder; it matters most for single-barrel bottles
- Three-quarter profile: a 45-degree view showing the bottle's overall shape and condition, useful for verifying authenticity on rarer expressions

Keep the camera stable for close-up capsule and lot-code shots. Blurred images obscure exactly the details you're trying to capture. Set your phone on a tripod or prop it against a stack of books.

## How do I photograph fill level accurately?

*Readers frequently ask* about this because fill-level grading is one of the primary factors affecting a bottle's secondary-market value. The mistake most collectors make is shooting from above, which makes a mid-shoulder fill look like an upper-shoulder fill.

The rule is simple: your camera lens must be level with the liquid surface. Place the bottle on a stable surface, crouch or lower your phone until the lens sits at exactly the height of the meniscus, and shoot straight across. Any upward angle inflates the apparent fill; any downward angle deflates it.

Use consistent side lighting, a window to the left or right of the bottle rather than behind you, so the light passes *through* the glass and makes the liquid surface clearly visible. If the bottle has dark glass, a backlight or light tent becomes necessary rather than optional. Once you establish a consistent fill-level angle, every bottle in your catalog becomes directly comparable, which matters when you're grading condition alongside our [guide to bourbon bottle condition grading](/articles/how-to-grade-bourbon-bottle-condition).

## How do I photograph a wax-dipped bottle without losing detail?

Wax-top bottles (Maker's Mark, Blanton's, Willett Family Estate, and many craft single barrels among them) present a specific challenge: the wax absorbs light and shows almost no surface detail under flat lighting. A raking side light, meaning a lamp positioned low and to the side, nearly parallel to the wax surface, reveals texture, drip patterns, and any imperfections that matter for condition grading.

For secondary-market purposes, photograph the wax from directly above as well as from the side. Buyers want to confirm the seal is unbroken and that the wax isn't chipped or cracked at the capsule edge. If you're cataloging a bottle you opened, a top-down shot of the cut wax is still worth keeping. It confirms how the bottle was opened and preserves a visual record.

## What settings actually matter on a smartphone?

Most collectors don't need to go deep into manual settings, but two adjustments consistently improve results:

- Lock focus and exposure separately. On iPhone, tap and hold on the label until the AF/AE lock banner appears. On Android, most camera apps let you tap to set focus and then drag a sun icon to adjust exposure independently. This prevents the camera from re-exposing every time you reframe.
- Shoot in the highest resolution your phone offers. Many phones default to a compressed format. Enable ProRAW (iPhone) or RAW/ultra-high-res mode if available. The difference is most visible when you crop in to read a batch code or lot number.

Shoot in natural daylight whenever possible. Color accuracy under incandescent or warm LED lighting skews the liquid amber-orange in ways that don't represent the bottle faithfully. If you must shoot indoors at night, set your phone's white balance manually to "Daylight" or use the coolest-temperature bulb you own.

## How should I name and organize the photos for my collection?

Consistent file naming is the difference between a searchable archive and a folder of 400 images called IMG_3847 through IMG_4246. A simple convention that works: `[Distillery]-[Expression]-[Year-Bottled]-[Angle].jpg`. For example: `BuffaloTrace-EHTaylor-SmallBatch-BiB-2023-front.jpg`.

If you're cataloging inside Pour Picks, photos attach directly to each bottle entry alongside your tasting notes, purchase price, and estimated current value, so the naming convention matters less since the app handles the metadata. For bottles stored in a general photo archive or a cloud folder as a backup, though, disciplined naming pays off quickly. Pair your photos with the [valuation data](/articles/how-to-value-a-bourbon-collection) you track so the record is complete: what you have, what it looks like, and what it's worth.

## FAQs

**What is the best background for photographing bourbon bottles?**
A matte white or light-gray backdrop is the safest all-purpose choice, it reflects light softly, keeps the bottle's color accurate, and makes the label easy to read. Dark or textured backgrounds can work aesthetically but hide label details that matter for documentation.

**Do I need a DSLR camera to photograph my bourbon collection?**
No. A modern smartphone camera, especially the 2023-or-later generation with computational photography, produces images detailed enough for cellar cataloging, insurance records, and secondary-market listings. A tripod or ProRAW mode will close the remaining gap with a DSLR for low-light situations.

**How do I avoid glare and reflections when photographing glass bourbon bottles?**
Diffuse your light source. Place a sheet of parchment paper over a lamp, shoot near a north-facing window on an overcast day, or use a collapsible light tent. Direct flash or an undiffused speedlight aimed straight at the bottle will almost always produce an unflattering white hotspot across the label.

**Which angles should I photograph for a secondary-market listing?**
Capture at least six shots: full front label, full back label, neck capsule or wax top-down, fill level from the side, lot or batch code (if visible on the bottom or neck), and one 45-degree three-quarter view showing the bottle shape. Buyers on secondary platforms use fill level and capsule condition to assess authenticity and value.

**How do I accurately photograph the fill level of a bourbon bottle?**
Shoot straight on at shoulder height so the camera lens is level with the liquid surface. Any upward or downward angle distorts the apparent fill. Use consistent side lighting so the meniscus is clearly visible, and note the fill grading term in your caption, upper shoulder, mid shoulder, and so on.
