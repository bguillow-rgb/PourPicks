Spend time on bourbon Reddit or in a bottle-shop conversation and you'll hear people talk about mash bills like everyone already knows what they mean. Then you look up Buffalo Trace and find references to "#1 mash bill" and "#2 mash bill" and realize the whole category has its own jargon.

A mash bill is the most predictive single piece of information about what's in a glass. Knowing how to read one tells you whether you'll like a bottle before you pour it.

## What is a mash bill?

A mash bill is the recipe of grains a distiller cooks together before fermentation. It's a list, usually three grains, with percentages. A standard bourbon mash bill might be:

- 75% corn
- 13% rye
- 12% malted barley

That percentage list is the entire definition. Everything else, the yeast, the water, the still, the barrel, the warehouse, comes after. The mash bill is the genetic code.

Grain choice has an outsized influence on final flavor. Two bourbons made by the same distillery, using the same yeast, water, still, and warehouse, will taste noticeably different if their mash bills differ. The same bourbon with a different yeast will taste similar but slightly different. The same bourbon with a different water source will taste almost identical.

When a writer mentions the mash bill, that's the most useful piece of information about what's in the glass.

## What grains are in a typical bourbon mash bill?

Bourbon legally has to be at least 51% corn. That's the only rule. The remaining 49% can be any other grain, though in practice almost every bourbon uses some combination of three:

**Corn (51-80% in most bourbons).** Provides natural sweetness, vanilla-caramel notes, and the round, soft body bourbon is known for. Higher corn percentage means a sweeter, smoother bourbon.

**Rye (5-35%).** The most common secondary grain. Provides spicy, peppery, slightly bitter notes that balance the corn sweetness. Higher rye content means more pronounced spice.

**Wheat (8-20%, when used instead of rye).** Used in wheated bourbons. Softer than rye; produces a gentler, more honey-bread character. Bourbons that use wheat don't also use rye. It's an either/or choice for the secondary grain.

**Malted barley (8-15%).** Almost every bourbon has some malted barley because it provides the enzymes that convert corn and rye starches into fermentable sugar. Barley contributes its own flavor too, biscuit, toast, sometimes a slight nuttiness, but it's mostly there to do work, not to be tasted.

A mash bill is almost always: 51-80% corn, 5-35% rye or wheat, 8-15% malted barley.

## What's the difference between a traditional, high-rye, and wheated mash bill?

These three categories cover virtually all bourbon you'll encounter:

| Style | Typical mash bill | Flavor profile | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Traditional (low-rye) bourbon | 75% corn / 13% rye / 12% barley | Balanced, sweet-forward, classic bourbon character | Buffalo Trace, Old Forester, Wild Turkey 101, Eagle Rare, Heaven Hill |
| High-rye bourbon | 60% corn / 28-35% rye / 5-12% barley | Spicier, drier; rye dominates the secondary palate | Four Roses Single Barrel (35% rye), Bulleit (28% rye), Old Grand-Dad (27% rye), Basil Hayden (27% rye), Woodford Reserve (~18% rye) |
| Wheated bourbon | 70% corn / 16% wheat / 14% barley | Softer, sweeter, less spice; honey-bread notes | Maker's Mark, Weller, Pappy Van Winkle, Larceny, Old Fitzgerald |

Those three patterns let you predict roughly 80% of what a bourbon will taste like before opening it.

## Why do mash bills explain so much about flavor?

Grains do what they do in any cooking: contribute their inherent flavors plus the byproducts of fermentation.

Fermenting corn produces compounds that read on the palate as vanilla, caramel, brown butter, baked apple. Fermenting rye produces compounds that read as pepper, cinnamon, mint, anise. Wheat fermentation produces gentler notes: toasted bread, honey, light malt.

Barrel aging amplifies and modifies all of this. New charred American oak adds caramel and vanilla that compound on top of the corn-derived sweetness. The char layer adds smoke, and the oak tannins add a drying effect.

The underlying skeleton, sweet vs. spicy, round vs. dry, soft vs. punchy, is set by the mash bill before the whiskey ever touches a barrel.

## Why do bourbons from the same distillery taste different?

Many distilleries run multiple mash bills.

Buffalo Trace Distillery runs four bourbon mash bills:
- **Mash bill #1** (low-rye, ~10% rye): used for Buffalo Trace, Eagle Rare, Stagg, George T. Stagg, E.H. Taylor
- **Mash bill #2** (high-rye, ~15% rye): used for Blanton's, Elmer T. Lee, Hancock's, Ancient Age
- **Wheated mash bill**: used for Weller, Pappy Van Winkle, Old Fitzgerald
- **BTAC wheated mash bill**: used for William Larue Weller

Same distillery, same water, same yeast strains, same warehouses, but four different bourbons because the mash bills differ. A fan of Buffalo Trace who doesn't love Blanton's, or the reverse, is probably tasting the difference between mash bill #1 and mash bill #2.

Heaven Hill, Wild Turkey, Maker's Mark, Brown-Forman, and most other major distilleries run multiple mash bills. Four Roses takes this furthest: two mash bills and five yeast strains, giving them 10 distinct bourbon recipes from one distillery.

## How do you find out a bourbon's mash bill?

Three places, in order of reliability:

**1. The distillery's website.** Some publish exact mash bills (Four Roses publishes all 10 of theirs). Most publish approximate percentages. A few publish nothing and rely on reviewer estimates.

**2. Specialized bourbon review sites.** Breaking Bourbon, Modern Thirst, and Whiskey Raiders maintain databases that include mash bills, often estimated from process disclosures. The estimates are usually correct within a percentage point or two.

**3. The bottle's TTB COLA filing.** Federal alcohol labeling rules require distilleries to disclose certain process details to the TTB. The COLA database is public and sometimes reveals mash bill percentages the distillery didn't put on the website. More work, but reliable.

For most drinkers, the distillery's published numbers plus a quick check on Breaking Bourbon or Whiskey Raiders is enough.

## Does mash bill matter more than age?

For bourbons under 12 years old, generally yes.

In the 4-10 year range, where most bourbon sits, the mash bill is the biggest predictor of how a bourbon will taste. A 6-year high-rye bourbon tastes like a high-rye bourbon. A 6-year wheated bourbon tastes like a wheated bourbon. The mash bill character dominates.

Past 12 years, age starts catching up. Long-aged bourbons take on so much oak character that mash bill differences narrow. Most 18-year bourbons taste similarly oaky and tannic regardless of mash bill. This is why ultra-aged bourbon often disappoints first-time buyers: they expected the mash bill they liked at 6 years, but at 18 years the oak has flattened the differences.

If you're learning bourbon, choose by mash bill style first, age second. A 4-year high-rye bourbon will teach you more about what you like than a 12-year of whatever mash bill happens to be on sale.

## What's the most useful mash bill knowledge for a beginner?

Memorize three benchmark bottles:

1. **Buffalo Trace** (~78% corn / 10% rye / 12% barley): low-rye traditional bourbon. This is your baseline; everything else is relative to it.
2. **Four Roses Single Barrel** (60% corn / 35% rye / 5% barley): high-rye bourbon. Notice the spicier finish.
3. **Maker's Mark** (70% corn / 16% wheat / 14% barley): wheated bourbon. Notice the absence of spice and the rounder sweetness.

Drink an ounce of each side-by-side. You'll learn more about mash bills in ten minutes than from reading another five articles. The differences are obvious when you're tasting them in parallel. They read as three genuinely different things, not three variations on the same theme.

After that tasting, every bourbon you encounter slots into one of those three buckets, plus or minus age and proof.
