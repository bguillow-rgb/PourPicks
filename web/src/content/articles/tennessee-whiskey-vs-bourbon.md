Tennessee whiskey and bourbon share almost identical DNA. Same grain rules, same barrel requirements, same American origin. Yet the two categories carry different names, different legal identities, and, if you pay close attention, meaningfully different flavors in the glass. For collectors who want to understand every bottle on the shelf, the distinction matters more than most label copy lets on.

## So wait, is Tennessee whiskey just bourbon with a different name?

*A question we hear often:* and the honest answer is almost, but not quite.

Tennessee whiskey must meet all the legal requirements of bourbon, plus more. It must be made in Tennessee, and it must use the Lincoln County Process: charcoal filtering before barreling.

That means every Tennessee whiskey is technically also a bourbon by federal definition, but not every bourbon is a Tennessee whiskey.

The reason it doesn't carry the bourbon label is partly legal, partly cultural. Jack Daniel's, along with many other Tennessee whiskey producers, actively avoids the term "bourbon." The category has its own identity, its own geography, and, most importantly for tasters, its own production step that changes what ends up in your glass.

## What exactly is the Lincoln County Process?

The Lincoln County Process, often called charcoal mellowing today, is the defining step that sets Tennessee whiskey apart from bourbon.

It involves filtering newly distilled whiskey through sugar maple charcoal before it's aged in barrels. In practice, that means dripping the whiskey through large vats packed with up to 10 feet of charcoal.

The timing matters: this happens *before* the spirit enters the barrel, while it's still raw new make. The charcoal vat filters out some of the vegetable oils and other compounds, mellowing the whiskey before it goes anywhere near a charred barrel.

Different distilleries execute this step differently. George Dickel uses vats that are 13 feet deep and lets the whiskey steep in the charcoal rather than trickle through, filling the vats entirely and using chill filtering. Jack Daniel's, by contrast, drips its spirit drop by drop through the charcoal column. Same basic principle, very different methods, and collectors who've tasted both side by side will tell you the results are noticeably different.

Alfred Eaton is generally credited with inventing the charcoal mellowing process in 1825, with the Lincoln County name coming from the county where the original Jack Daniel's distillery was located. Jack Daniel's began in Lincoln County, though the distillery now sits in Moore County, which was carved out of Lincoln County in 1871. The name stuck even after the geography changed.

## Does the Lincoln County Process actually change how the whiskey tastes?

*This one comes up a lot:* especially from collectors who wonder whether the step is meaningful or mostly marketing.

The short answer is yes, but not in the way most people assume. Writers in books from major publishers and prominent magazines have incorrectly stated that the Lincoln County Process adds sweet maple flavors to the whiskey. That is a persistent myth. The process doesn't add flavor or color. Instead, it removes harsher grain notes and impurities, producing a smoother, more approachable spirit that lets the barrel character come through more cleanly.

The net effect on flavor is real but subtle. Because of the charcoal filtering, Tennessee whiskey tends to be sweeter, mellower, and softer on the palate than an equivalent bourbon. Bourbon typically runs toward bold vanilla, oak, and spice; Tennessee whiskey leans lighter, with hints of caramel and grain. On the finish, bourbon often packs a punch, while Tennessee whiskey usually finishes cleaner and sweeter.

## What rules do both categories share?

The overlap is substantial. Here's a side-by-side look at the legal requirements:

| Requirement | Bourbon | Tennessee Whiskey |
|---|---|---|
| Made in the United States | Required | Required (+ must be Tennessee) |
| Mash bill 51% corn | Required | Required |
| Aged in new charred oak barrels | Required | Required |
| Distilled to 160 proof | Required | Required |
| Barreled at 125 proof | Required | Required |
| Bottled at 80 proof | Required | Required |
| Lincoln County Process | Not required | Required (with one exception) |
| Minimum age | None (straight = 2 yrs) | None (though most are 4+ yrs) |

Both must have a mash bill of at least 51% corn, both must age in new charred oak barrels, and neither requires a minimum age. Although most Tennessee whiskeys are at least four years old, those younger than that require an age statement, and the same goes for bourbon.

## Is there any Tennessee whiskey that skips the Lincoln County Process?

*Readers frequently ask:* and yes, there's one notable carve-out.

Not every producer labeled as Tennessee whiskey uses the process. Benjamin Prichard's Tennessee Whiskey is the exception. Prichard's holds a grandfathering provision that exempts it from the Lincoln County Process requirement while still allowing the Tennessee whiskey label.

This is worth knowing for collectors chasing completism or building comparative flights. A Prichard's expression alongside a Jack Daniel's single barrel and a George Dickel barrel select makes for a genuinely interesting tasting: three "Tennessee whiskeys," three meaningfully different production paths. That kind of [vertical tasting](/articles/what-is-a-bourbon-vertical-tasting) logic applies just as well across state lines as it does across age statements.

## Does Tennessee whiskey have a secondary market?

It does, though it's smaller and less data-rich than the bourbon secondary. According to whiskey auction data tracked through platforms like Bourbon Fans and Whiskey Auctioneer, limited George Dickel single-barrel releases and Uncle Nearest 1856 premium expressions have shown meaningful price appreciation over retail in recent years, particularly bottles from 2020-2022 before the category gained broader collector attention.

Many enthusiasts have shifted toward "worth it" bottles: spirits that balance flavor, maturity, and value over hype, with serious quality at a justifiable price rather than scarcity alone. Tennessee whiskey fits neatly into that value-forward collecting mindset for 2026. The bottles are generally easier to find than allocated Kentucky releases, the flavor profile is distinct enough to anchor a collection, and the limited single-barrel expressions from both major and craft producers have room to appreciate.

If you're already tracking your bourbon cellar's value, adding Tennessee expressions to your inventory makes sense. They deserve the same condition grading, provenance notes, and fill-level documentation you'd give any serious bottle. ## How should collectors think about buying Tennessee whiskey?

For most collectors, Tennessee whiskey is best approached as a distinct category worthy of deliberate inclusion, not an afterthought, and not a substitute for bourbon. A practical framework:

For tasting flights, a Tennessee whiskey slot in any [bourbon flight](/articles/how-to-build-a-bourbon-flight) built around mash bill or flavor profile creates a useful reference point. The charcoal-mellowed baseline shows clearly what the Lincoln County Process removes and, by contrast, what barrel aging alone contributes in an equivalent Kentucky expression.

For cellar depth, single-barrel Tennessee whiskey releases, particularly Dickel's store picks and the growing Uncle Nearest limited lineup, offer proof-point and age variety that complements allocated Kentucky bottles without competing in the same scarcity bracket.

For understanding labels, because Tennessee whiskey meets federal bourbon standards, you can apply everything you already know about [reading a bourbon label](/articles/how-to-read-a-bourbon-label), including proof, age statement, and distillery number, to Tennessee bottles. The one addition to watch for is any mention of the Lincoln County Process, which confirms the production method even when the label doesn't spell it out explicitly.

The Kentucky Distillers' Association has reported that Kentucky now holds a record 16.1 million barrels of bourbon aging in its warehouses, with more than 125 licensed distilleries operating, the most since Prohibition's repeal. Tennessee's barrel inventory is smaller but growing, with the state's craft distillery count rising steadily through 2025 and 2026. More producers means more single-barrel releases, and more releases means more opportunities for collectors who pay attention early.

## FAQs

**Is Jack Daniel's a bourbon?**
Technically, Jack Daniel's meets all the federal legal requirements to be called bourbon, but the brand actively avoids that label. Because it undergoes the Lincoln County Process and is produced in Tennessee, it is marketed and legally classified as Tennessee whiskey, not bourbon.

**Does the Lincoln County Process add maple flavor to Tennessee whiskey?**
No, this is a persistent myth. The charcoal mellowing step removes harsh grain compounds and vegetable oils; it does not impart maple or sweet flavors. Any sweetness you taste comes from the corn mash and barrel aging, the same sources as in bourbon.

**Can bourbon be made in Tennessee?**
Yes. Bourbon can legally be produced anywhere in the United States, including Tennessee. A Tennessee distillery could choose to make a straight bourbon without the Lincoln County Process, it simply couldn't call that product Tennessee whiskey under state law.

**Which Tennessee whiskey brands skip the Lincoln County Process?**
Benjamin Prichard's is the notable exception. It holds a grandfather provision under Tennessee's 2013 law that exempts it from the Lincoln County Process requirement while still legally labeling its product as Tennessee whiskey.

**Does Tennessee whiskey belong in a bourbon collector's cellar?**
Absolutely. Single-barrel and limited Tennessee whiskey releases, particularly George Dickel barrel selections and Uncle Nearest expressions, have real secondary-market traction and offer a flavor profile worth documenting alongside your bourbon bottles.
