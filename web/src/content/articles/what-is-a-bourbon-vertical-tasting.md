A vertical tasting puts several releases from the same distillery side-by-side so you can taste how character evolves across age, proof, or production year. If you've been cataloging your cellar and wondering what to do with multiple releases from the same distillery, this is the format worth knowing.

## What exactly is a bourbon vertical tasting?

A vertical tasting puts several releases from the same distillery side-by-side so you can taste how their character evolves across age, proof, or production year. "To me, a vertical is tasting the different expressions produced by the same distillery," in the words of experienced tasting room operators. You're looking to identify similarities and differences within those expressions. The goal isn't to declare a winner; it's to understand what a single distillery's DNA looks and tastes like when you strip away everything except time and wood.

Contrast that with a **horizontal tasting**: a vertical covers products from the same distillery (several kinds of Four Roses, for instance) while a horizontal covers the same type of bourbon from different distilleries, like three different ryes. Verticals teach you one house's character in depth. Horizontals teach you how the category varies across producers. Both are worth doing, and most collectors find verticals more immediately useful when building a focused cellar.

## Okay, but what's the *point* for someone who already collects?

*A question we hear often:* if you're already tracking your bottles and writing tasting notes, why bother organizing a formal vertical?

Tasting in isolation and tasting comparatively are different experiences. When you nose a 10-year expression next to the 15-year from the same mash bill, your brain has a reference point it didn't have before. You stop describing flavors in the abstract and start describing change: the way oak integration deepens, how a fruit note softens or disappears, why the higher-proof release feels bolder on the finish.

Verticals also sharpen your ability to evaluate future releases before you buy. If you've done a thorough Four Roses vertical and have a precise sense of what their low-rye mash bill tastes like at 10, 12, and 15 years, you have a real benchmark when a new limited release hits shelves. You're extrapolating from firsthand data rather than guessing. Pour Picks' tasting journal is a natural home for those comparative notes; tagging entries by distillery makes it easy to pull up your full vertical history before a purchase decision.

## How is a vertical different from just opening a bunch of bottles?

The structure matters more than most people expect.

Start with the lowest-proof bourbon and work up to the highest, so your palate doesn't get overwhelmed before you reach the subtler bottles. Beyond proof order, keep pour size consistent: one ounce per expression is standard, enough to evaluate nose, palate, and finish without rushing. Five or six bottles is a workable range, enough variety to reveal meaningful patterns without going overboard.

Have still water and something plain and starchy between pours; unsalted crackers work well. Opening all bottles about half an hour beforehand lets them breathe. And resist the urge to check tasting notes before you've written your own. Pre-reading colors your perception in ways that make the exercise less useful.

## Vertical vs. horizontal: which format is right for my collection?

| Format | What you compare | What you learn | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Vertical** | Multiple expressions from one distillery | Distillery DNA, effect of age/proof on a single mash bill | Collectors focused on one producer or building cellar depth |
| **Horizontal** | Same style across multiple distilleries | How grain, process, and region shape a category | Collectors exploring a new style (e.g., wheated bourbon, high-rye) |
| **Blind vertical** | Same as vertical, labels hidden | Honest sensory evaluation without price/label bias | Building palate confidence, settling a real debate |
| **Annual release vertical** | Same product across multiple vintage years | Year-to-year variation, distillery consistency | Collectors who buy multiples of the same annual release |

The best format is whichever question you're trying to answer.

## What distilleries make the best starting verticals?

*Readers frequently ask:* where do I even begin if I haven't done this before?

The best verticals come from distilleries with clearly differentiated expressions built on a consistent mash bill. If the expressions are too similar, you spend the evening confirming they taste alike. If they're too different (different mash bills, widely different proof), you're running a horizontal in disguise.

Look for a distillery with a focused portfolio that offers clear variation between expressions. Old Forester's Whiskey Row Series or Bardstown Bourbon Company's Origin Series are strong examples. For collectors who already have access to annual releases, comparing different years from the same distillery is a useful format, and it's especially effective with single-barrel bottlings, where each release carries a specific barrel number and dump date.

Single barrel releases give you a precise foothold when comparing across a vertical. That's an edge over blended annual releases, where year-to-year differences can be subtle by design. "Year to year your distillate should be consistent before it goes into the barrel," as one master distiller explains, but "we're working with Mother Nature, so you're always going to get little different nuances." Those nuances are what a well-structured vertical is designed to surface.

A few starting lineups worth considering:

- **Elijah Craig**: standard small batch, barrel proof (any batch), and 18-year; three distinct points on one mash bill
- **Four Roses**: OBSK, OESO, and Small Batch Select reveals how yeast and grain interact
- **Old Forester Whiskey Row**: 1870, 1897, 1910, and 1920 offer proof and process variation in a clean sequence
- **Pinhook Vertical Series**: purpose-built for the format; the concept behind the series is to minimize year-to-year variables by using an identical mash bill, resulting in nearly identical products with just one primary difference in how the proof is adjusted to best complement the resultant whiskey.

## Does adding a drop of water change anything?

Yes, and intentionally so. "A drop or two of water should be added after an initial taste neat, as the chemical reaction will boost the concentrations of flavor compounds and 'open' or 'release' aromatics to further explore the depth of the whiskey."

In a vertical, this becomes especially revealing. The younger or lower-proof expression may open dramatically with water while the higher-proof aged release barely changes, or vice versa. Tasting each expression neat first, then with a few drops of water, doubles your data points and often surfaces a second layer of comparisons you'd have missed otherwise.

## How do I document a vertical so I actually remember what I tasted?

*Readers frequently ask:* tasting notes made during a vertical feel different from solo session notes. There's more context, more contrast, and more to capture.

The most useful vertical notes record not just what each expression smells and tastes like in isolation, but how it differs from the others in the flight. Write a brief comparison after each pour: "fruitier and less oaky than the 10-year; the rye spice that was subtle there becomes the dominant note here." Those relational observations are what a vertical is positioned to generate, and they're more useful than standalone descriptors when you're deciding whether to acquire the next release in the series.

Keep pour size consistent and log the order you tasted so you can account for palate fatigue when you review your notes later. A vertical tasting samples multiple ages of the same brand to isolate the effect of barrel aging; a horizontal samples different mash bills at similar ages and proofs to reveal how grain changes spice, sweetness, and mouthfeel. Your notes should reflect which variable you were actually testing. Logging verticals inside Pour Picks' journal and linking each entry to the bottle in your cellar keeps the full picture connected. You can see your tasting history alongside each bottle's acquisition date, purchase price, and current estimated value.

## What's happening in the collector market that makes verticals more relevant now?

The bourbon resale market is maturing. Collectors are becoming more selective about what deserves a premium. Instead of chasing scarce bottles, many have shifted toward bourbons that balance flavor, maturity, and value over hype.

The clearest market theme right now is bifurcation: ultra-aged and cask strength expressions have dominated gains, with several aged expressions surging significantly, reflecting renewed collector appetite for rare, high-proof aged releases with demonstrable scarcity. Collectors who have done thorough verticals within a distillery's lineup are better positioned to identify which specific expressions in a series represent genuine quality step-ups, and which are just priced that way.

The secondary market has moved away from social media hype toward data-driven auction results. Collectors who combine hands-on vertical tasting with real pricing data, the kind available through cellar tracking tools, are making buying and holding decisions on evidence rather than reputation.
