Every bourbon bottle tells you who made it, if you know the one line to read. That line isn't the brand name on the front. It isn't the distillery photo on the back neck. It's the small production statement, usually near the address, that uses one of four phrases: *distilled by*, *produced by*, *bottled by*, or *produced for*. Each phrase carries a distinct legal meaning under TTB regulations. Collectors who learn to parse them quickly will never be surprised by a sourced bottle again.

## Why does the phrasing on a bourbon label actually matter legally?

Under TTB regulations, only someone who distilled the whiskey can use the term "distilled by" to identify the source of the whiskey. That makes it the strongest claim a label can make: it pins a specific licensed facility to the liquid in the bottle. Everything else is a step down the transparency ladder.

The term "produced by," when applied to distilled spirits, does not refer to the original distillation of the spirits but instead indicates a processing operation that involves a change in the class or type of the product through the addition of flavors or some other processing activity. In plain language, "produced by" is a rectifier's term. The entity named may have blended, filtered, finished, or simply re-bottled the bourbon, but the phrase carries no legal obligation to have run a still.

The TTB acknowledges that although the phrases "bottled by" and "distilled by" are unambiguous, the meaning of other commonly-used terms such as "produced by," "made by," and "manufactured by" may not be entirely clear. That ambiguity is exactly why collectors get caught off guard.

## What does each label phrase actually mean, and what does it not tell me?

*A question we hear often:* collectors want a simple cheat sheet. Here it is:

| Label phrase | Who is named? | Distillation claim? | Common scenario |
|---|---|---|---|
| Distilled by | The actual distiller | Yes, strongest claim | Distillery sells under its own brand |
| Produced by | A rectifier / processor | No | Sourced or finished bourbon rebranded |
| Bottled by | The bottler | No | Could be the distiller or an NDP |
| Made by / Manufactured by | Processor | No | Marketing language, no distillation claim |
| Produced for | The commissioning buyer | No | Private label, retailer picks, white-label brands |

"Distilled by" means the company named actually made the liquid. "Bottled by" means they put it in glass, nothing more. And "produced by," the one that sounds the most impressive, is legally a processing claim: blending, flavoring, finishing. It is not a claim that anybody distilled anything.

One nuance worth knowing: instead of using any of these terms, the term "bottled by" can be used by distillers or rectifiers alike. So "bottled by" is genuinely ambiguous. It could be a legitimate distillery that chose conservative label language, or it could be an NDP that bought someone else's juice.

## So if "produced by" shows up, does that mean the bourbon is sourced?

*This one comes up a lot.* The short answer: probably yes, but not always.

"Distilled by" means just that, whereas "produced by" means the whiskey was bottled by another party than the distiller. Companies do make mistakes, and usually the mistake is the distiller using "produced by" as opposed to a bottler using "distilled by," but it goes both ways. If a distillery has used "produced by" across its whole portfolio forever, they may simply not have updated their label language. But if a brand uses "distilled by" on its vodka and "produced by" on its bourbon, they know the difference, and they didn't distill the whiskey.

The practical rule most serious collectors use: if the label doesn't say "distilled by," assume it's sourced. Look for the D word. If you don't see it, chances are they didn't make it.

That said, sourced doesn't mean bad. A sourced bourbon, purchased in bulk from a large distillery and bottled under a smaller brand's label, is not lower quality by definition. Many good bourbons are sourced. The label language just tells you what you're actually drinking, and the collector who knows it can make an informed choice either way.

## How does the DSP number help me verify the actual distillery?

Even when label language is ambiguous, the DSP number rarely lies. DSP stands for Distilled Spirits Plant, the federal license number assigned to every facility that produces, stores, or processes distilled spirits in the United States.

The DSP number is prefixed by state. A DSP beginning with "KY-" is a Kentucky facility; one starting with "IN-" points to Indiana. If the front label says "Crafted in the heart of Appalachia" but the DSP resolves to Indiana, you're almost certainly looking at MGP-sourced liquid.

You can cross-reference DSP numbers against the TTB's publicly searchable COLA (Certificate of Label Approval) registry online. A bonded label has to carry two plant numbers: the DSP where it was distilled, and the one where it was bottled. If they match, one house made that whiskey start to finish. If they don't, somebody bottled somebody else's work. Perfectly legal, and the label just told you. Even on non-bonded bottles, the DSP is a useful cross-reference tool.

If you're logging bottles in an app like [Pour Picks](/bourbon-inventory-app), noting the DSP in your tasting notes is a low-effort habit that pays off when you're trying to trace why two differently branded bottles taste suspiciously similar.

## What about "Kentucky Straight Bourbon Whiskey" -- does the state in the name lock in where it was distilled?

*Readers frequently ask:* does labeling a bourbon "Kentucky Straight" guarantee it was distilled in Kentucky?

Yes, and this is one case where the full class-and-type designation does the work for you. If a whiskey is labeled "Kentucky Straight Bourbon Whiskey," it must have been both distilled and aged in Kentucky. If the label just says "Straight Bourbon Whiskey" with a Kentucky address, the distillation may have occurred elsewhere.

That dropped "Kentucky" from the class designation is a quiet but real difference. A bottle labeled simply "Straight Bourbon Whiskey" with a Bardstown, KY address could have been distilled in Tennessee, Indiana, or anywhere else in the country. The producer is headquartered in Kentucky; the liquid may not be. This is why collectors who understand [how to read a bourbon label](/articles/how-to-read-a-bourbon-label) treat the full class-and-type statement, not the address, as the authoritative geographic claim.

According to the TTB's own labeling standards, including the state name in the class designation (e.g., "Kentucky Bourbon Whiskey" vs. "Bourbon Whiskey") triggers geographic authenticity requirements. Roughly 95% of all bourbon is produced in Kentucky, but the remaining 5% is a growing category, and sourced brands sometimes lean on Kentucky imagery without Kentucky distillation.

## What is "produced for," and where does it appear?

"Produced for" is the phrase you'll most often find on private-label and retailer-exclusive bottles. It names the commissioning entity, the retailer, restaurant group, or brand that ordered the release, not the facility that made it.

Read the responsibility lines, "distilled by," "bottled by," "produced for," to understand who did what. Treat terms like "small batch," "private select," and "full proof" as marketing unless the label provides specific, verifiable details.

A bottle saying "Produced for ABC Wine & Spirits, Nashville, TN" simply means ABC commissioned it. The distillery that actually made it may or may not appear elsewhere on the label. Some high-quality [store picks](/articles/what-is-a-bourbon-store-pick) and [private barrel programs](/articles/what-is-a-bourbon-private-barrel-program) use this language because the retailer, not the distillery, holds the label approval. In those cases, look for a "distilled by" statement or DSP number elsewhere on the back label to identify the actual producer.

## Does any of this tell me whether the bourbon is actually good?

Honestly? Not directly. The label tells you about provenance and transparency, both of which matter for [valuing a collection](/articles/how-to-value-a-bourbon-collection) and for [spotting fakes](/articles/how-to-spot-a-fake-bourbon-bottle), but neither "distilled by" nor "produced by" predicts flavor. Some of the most celebrated bottles in the secondary market, including several MGP-distilled releases that trade well above MSRP, carry "produced by" language from the NDP that bottled them.

What the language does tell you is whether the brand is being straight with you. A brand that chooses "distilled by" when it could have hedged with "produced by" is signaling confidence in its provenance story. The small production statement tells you something about the work. "Distilled by" and "bottled by" are worth reading, especially when a bottle's local story matters to you, but neither phrase is a complete biography of the liquid.

The full picture requires the DSP number, the state-of-distillation claim, cross-referencing with the TTB COLA database, and, for older bottles, a working knowledge of [how to date a vintage bottle](/articles/how-to-date-a-vintage-bourbon-bottle) and verify authenticity. Used together, these tools give you a complete provenance picture no front-label story can fake.
