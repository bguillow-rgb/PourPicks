How to Spot a Fake Bourbon Bottle

Counterfeit bourbon is a real secondary-market risk. Learn the 7 authentication checks every collector should run before buying a rare bottle.

By Pour Picks · Published September 13, 2026

In brief

Spot a fake bourbon bottle by checking five physical markers in order: the tax strip or neck seal for signs of removal or re-gluing, label print quality for blurring or font errors, laser-etched lot codes for mismatches, fill level for signs of re-topping, and bottle weight, since counterfeits are often lighter than originals.

Buying a rare bourbon through the secondary market is exciting right up until doubt creeps in. The seal looks slightly off. The fill level is higher than you remember on your last bottle. The lot code doesn’t quite match the reference photo you saved.

Demand for bourbon keeps climbing, and so does the sophistication of the people faking it. Collectors are losing real money.

This guide is for collectors who buy, trade, or gift rare bottles through unofficial channels. It covers the physical and procedural checks that separate an authentic pour from a well-packaged fraud.

Why is counterfeit bourbon a collector’s problem and not just a retail problem?

A question we hear often: if you’re buying from a reputable store, do you even need to worry about this?

Short answer: almost never at retail, but frequently everywhere else.

If you’re buying from a licensed liquor store, the chances of running into a fake are essentially zero. The U.S. three-tier system (producers, distributors, retailers) makes it very hard for counterfeit products to slip through. The problem lives almost entirely in the secondary market, among collectors trading rare bottles or vintage dusties through unofficial channels. Everyday drinkers shopping at the liquor store have nothing to worry about. Collectors chasing unicorn bottles do.

Bourbon fraud rarely looks like vineyard mislabeling. It looks like scarcity manipulation. The bourbon boom created the right conditions: limited releases, social media hype, private trading groups, massive markups, and buyers desperate to find Pappy Van Winkle, Weller, Blanton’s, or E.H. Taylor. High price, passionate buyer, informal setting. That’s exactly where counterfeits thrive.

What are the two main types of counterfeit bourbon bottles?

This one comes up a lot: collectors often assume a fake bottle means a fake label slapped on completely different liquid. The reality is more nuanced.

Whiskey counterfeiting breaks down into two methods. The first is manufacturing a cheap product from scratch and passing it off as the real thing. The second, and increasingly common, approach is refilling an authentic bottle with inferior liquid. The refill method is harder to catch, even for experienced collectors and some of the oldest names in the business.

Refilling is now the dominant tactic in the bourbon world, largely because genuine empty bottles are easy to find. A bad actor acquires an empty Pappy 23, sources cheap commodity whiskey, refills it, re-seals it, and lists it at a 30% “deal” designed to attract exactly the kind of motivated buyer who won’t ask too many questions. The bottle itself is real. The liquid isn’t. That distinction matters, because the inspection checklist differs depending on which type of fake you’re dealing with. A fully fabricated bottle fails label tests first. A refilled genuine bottle often passes label tests but fails fill-level and lot-code checks.

Which bourbon expressions are most frequently targeted by counterfeiters?

Rare allocated brands like Colonel E.H. Taylor and Pappy Van Winkle are the most common targets, with fakes cropping up on websites and in social media trading groups. Beyond those two, the Weller lineup has become an increasingly attractive target as prices for the wheated bourbon line have risen. Weller 12 Year Old and Weller Antique 107 are the most commonly faked expressions in that family.

A useful rule of thumb: any bottle whose secondary-market price exceeds retail by a factor of three or more is a counterfeiting target. A Pappy Van Winkle sold at or near retail in the secondary market is almost certainly fake. The “deal” is the tell, not the prize.

How do I check the tax strip and neck seal?

Readers frequently ask: what’s the very first thing to examine before anything else?

Start at the top of the bottle. For bottles old enough to carry a federal tax strip (generally pre-1986 American releases), counterfeiters often transfer a genuine tax strip from an old wine bottle to cover up a refill. Feel carefully around the neck for sticky patches, which can indicate where an original strip was removed. On a genuine vintage bottle, a chip in the label won’t leave any glue residue behind.

For modern releases, examine the capsule or wax seal under good light. Look for:

  • Glue seepage or a ghost line around the base of the capsule, which signals it was removed and re-applied
  • Color inconsistency in the wax or foil relative to reference images
  • Cork hardware that doesn’t match the brand’s standard (a Blanton’s bottle should have its recognizable stopper; a generic synthetic cork is a red flag)
  • A capsule that spins freely (authentic heat-shrink capsules grip the neck)

What do I look for on the label?

Labels are the easiest thing for an experienced eye to read. Genuine bourbon bottles are printed on commercial presses with high-resolution artwork, specific fonts, matched colors, and precise textures. Fakes use cheaper materials or inconsistent printing, and it usually shows.

Run through this checklist in order:

  1. Resolution and crispness: hold the label at an angle under a single light source. Blurring, pixelation, or fuzzy serif edges are disqualifying.
  2. Font matching: compare against a verified reference image. Counterfeiters frequently use the closest publicly available digital font, which is never quite right. Pay close attention to kerning on the distillery name.
  3. Color saturation: genuine labels use Pantone-matched inks. A label that looks washed out or slightly off compared to your reference is suspicious.
  4. Embossing and foil elements: many allocated releases include micro-embossing or hot-stamp foil detail that is very difficult to replicate cheaply. Run a fingernail lightly across any foil element; it should be crisply raised.
  5. Glue pattern under the label edges: carefully lift a corner edge on a bottle you own or are inspecting in person. An authentic label is applied by machine with even glue coverage. A replacement label often shows uneven application or dried excess adhesive.

Counterfeit bottles frequently show blurry labels, mismatched or missing laser codes, and unusual glue patterns. And if the price seems too good to be true for a rare bottle, that’s part of the picture too.

How do lot codes and laser etching work, and how do I use them?

This is the most technically reliable check available to collectors today.

Look for laser codes on the glass. Counterfeits often have mismatched or scrubbed codes. The code typically includes the date and time of bottling, and counterfeiters rarely get this right. They may refill an old empty that still carries an old code, or they may try to scrub the code off entirely. A “new” bottle with a laser code from several years prior, or no code at all, is worth walking away from.

For Buffalo Trace family bottles specifically, check for laser-etched codes on the glass indicating distillery, warehouse, barrel, and date information. Newer bottles may also include NFC technology for smartphone verification through the Buffalo Trace app.

You can cross-reference the DSP (Distilled Spirits Plant) number printed on every label against the TTB’s public COLA (Certificate of Label Approval) registry at ttb.gov. A mismatched or missing DSP number on a bottled-in-bond bottle is a red flag. This check takes about 90 seconds and costs nothing.

If you’re actively cataloging your collection, keep a photograph of each bottle’s lot code in your records. The Pour Picks bourbon inventory app lets you attach photos directly to each bottle entry, so you always have a dated, timestamped reference on hand, useful both for insurance documentation and for comparing a bottle you’re evaluating against one you already own.

What does fill level tell me about a bottle’s authenticity?

Readers frequently ask: is a high fill level actually suspicious, or is that just how some bottles come?

Fill level (called ullage when referring to the airspace in a bottle) is a meaningful but nuanced signal. On release, most bottles are filled to the mid-neck, though some releases are filled only to the shoulder, and ullage can increase over time as a bottle ages.

What this means practically: a sealed bottle with a fill level above the standard mid-neck position for that expression should raise suspicion, not comfort. A refiller who tops off a partially empty bottle often over-fills it. Compare the fill level against verified reference images for the specific expression and vintage year. A bottle of Pappy 23 with liquid right up to the capsule on a 10-year-old release deserves a closer look. Our article on what is bourbon bottle ullage walks through how to read fill levels accurately for both vintage and modern releases.

How do I verify a bottle I’m about to buy through the secondary market?

This one comes up a lot: what’s the practical workflow before committing to a high-dollar secondary purchase?

Run these steps in sequence before money changes hands.

Step 1: demand high-resolution photos. At minimum: front label, back label, neck seal top-down, lot code and laser etching close-up, and fill level side-on. Any seller unwilling to provide all five is not worth engaging.

Step 2: price-check against the market. Check sites like Bottle Blue Book for reference. If the price is too good to be true, it very likely is. Scammers prey specifically on newer collectors who want a deal.

Step 3: verify the seller’s platform and history. Licensed auction houses (Skinner, Bonhams, Hart Davis Hart) have legal liability for what they sell and real vetting processes. Peer-to-peer groups on social media have neither.

Step 4: contact the distillery if uncertain. Most distilleries have a process for verifying bottles, particularly rare and collectible releases. Use it.

Step 5: inspect physically before full payment if possible. Fakes are often housed in lighter bottles, so pay attention to your first impression when you pick it up. Authentic bourbon bottles are made from thick, heavy glass. A noticeably light bottle for its size is a warning sign.

What should I do with empty bottles from my collection?

Serious collectors take this seriously. Once you’ve finished a bottle, dispose of it completely unless you’re keeping it for display. Many Pappy Van Winkle buyers make a point of carefully breaking bottles before disposal. Buffalo Trace Distillery asks buyers to take similar precautions, suggesting scoring or marring the label before recycling the glass.

An intact, authenticated empty is raw material for a refill operation. A cracked or deliberately marred bottle is not. Keeping display empties is fine. Making it easy for someone to re-use them downstream is not.

Frequently Asked Questions

How common are fake bourbon bottles on the secondary market?

Counterfeits are concentrated in the highest-demand allocated expressions, Pappy Van Winkle, Weller, Buffalo Trace Antique Collection, and Colonel E.H. Taylor. Rare Whisky 101 has estimated that counterfeit bottles account for a significant share of premium whiskey resold through informal channels. Risk is near-zero at licensed retailers but meaningfully elevated in peer-to-peer trades and social-media groups.

What are the most commonly faked bourbon brands?

Pappy Van Winkle, the Weller lineup (especially Weller 12 and Weller Antique 107), Buffalo Trace Antique Collection expressions, Colonel E.H. Taylor, and Blanton's are the most frequently counterfeited bourbons because their secondary-market prices make the effort worthwhile for bad actors.

Can I verify a bourbon bottle's authenticity with the distillery?

Yes. Most major distilleries, including Buffalo Trace, Brown-Forman, and Heaven Hill, will verify bottles when contacted directly. Bring or photograph the lot code, DSP number, and any neck codes before reaching out. Some newer expressions also support NFC chip or QR-code verification through brand apps.

What does a re-topped or refilled bourbon bottle look like?

Look for a fill level that sits higher than the mid-neck standard for that release, sticky residue or discoloration where a capsule was removed, a cork that doesn't match the brand's hardware, and a tax strip with glue seepage or a faint ghost line where an older strip was removed and repositioned.

Is it safe to drink from a bottle I suspect might be fake?

No. A refilled bottle could contain anything from cheap commodity whiskey to genuinely unsafe liquids. If you have serious doubts about a bottle's contents, not just its label, do not consume it. Contact the distillery, a trusted auction house, or local authorities if you believe fraud occurred.
