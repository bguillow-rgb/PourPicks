What Is the Buffalo Trace Antique Collection (BTAC)? Every Bottle, Release, and Collector Decision Explained

BTAC is five annual limited-release whiskeys from Buffalo Trace Distillery — three bourbons, two ryes — bottled at barrel proof. Here's every expression explained.

By Pour Picks · Published October 23, 2026

In brief

The Buffalo Trace Antique Collection (BTAC) is an annual fall release of five limited-edition whiskeys from Buffalo Trace Distillery in Frankfort, Kentucky. The lineup includes three bourbons and two rye whiskeys, all bottled at or near barrel proof, unfiltered, and released in extremely small quantities each year.

The Buffalo Trace Antique Collection is the annual release that stops bourbon collectors cold every fall. Five bottles, sometimes six, appear in tiny quantities at retail, vanish within hours, and command multiples of their MSRP on the secondary market almost immediately. If you’ve been hearing the acronym “BTAC” and wondered what the fuss is about, this guide breaks down every expression, the mechanics of the release, what the bottles actually taste like, and how to think about them as a collector.

Okay, so what actually is BTAC?

The Buffalo Trace Antique Collection, known universally as BTAC, is an annual release of five limited-edition expressions from Buffalo Trace Distillery in Frankfort, Kentucky. They are among the most anticipated and most allocated whiskey releases of the year, every year.

What sets BTAC apart is that these whiskeys are bottled at barrel proof or close to it, with no chill filtration. You get every bit of flavor and character that years of aging developed. For collectors who care about how a whiskey is made, not just how rare it is, that philosophy matters. You’re getting the barrel’s honest output rather than a proofed-down, filtered product engineered for consistency.

Since its launch in 2000, the Antique Collection has grown from a trilogy into the lineup we know today. Thomas H. Handy was the last expression added, joining the series in 2006. That 26-year run is part of what gives BTAC its weight: the releases have enough history to show how a distillery’s house character evolves across decades, which makes earlier vintages genuinely interesting material for vertical tastings.

What are the five bottles, and how do they differ?

This one comes up a lot: collectors new to BTAC often assume the bottles are interchangeable prestige pours. They’re not. Each expression is built on a different mash bill, aging timeline, or proof philosophy.

The five expressions are George T. Stagg, William Larue Weller, Thomas H. Handy Sazerac Rye, Sazerac 18 Year Old Rye, and Eagle Rare 17 Year Old.

The 2025 George T. Stagg clocked in at 142.8 proof, strong enough that collectors nicknamed it “Hazmat.” That annual variation in proof is a defining BTAC feature. No two vintages of Stagg or Weller drink exactly alike, and tracking those year-over-year differences is a significant part of what makes collecting the series interesting.

Why does proof change every year?

Readers frequently ask: if Buffalo Trace has the same recipe and barrels every cycle, why isn’t BTAC consistent?

The answer comes down to how barrel-proof bottling works. During aging, evaporation removes water or alcohol at different rates depending on warehouse position, ambient heat, and seasonal temperature swings, which is why rickhouse floor position affects bourbon flavor so dramatically. By the time a barrel is selected for BTAC, its proof is whatever the barrel itself produced. Buffalo Trace doesn’t dilute and doesn’t adjust. What’s in the barrel goes in the bottle.

George T. Stagg is uncut, unfiltered bourbon aged for around 15 years and bottled straight from the barrel. Proof changes every year, and it’s often enormous. For collectors who log their bottles carefully, noting the vintage year alongside the proof is the only way to compare what you drank accurately against future pours or against other collectors’ notes. Apps like Pour Picks let you capture that kind of per-bottle metadata (vintage, proof, purchase date, tasting notes) so it’s never lost.

Which BTAC bottle is the best?

This is the wrong question, but it’s the right argument to have with other collectors. The five expressions represent fundamentally different experiences rather than a ranked hierarchy.

If you prefer richer, smoother sweetness, William Larue Weller is usually the better fit. It still has strength, but it leans more polished and generous than aggressive. If you value age, balance, and a slower kind of complexity, Eagle Rare 17 and Sazerac 18 are the bottles to watch. These are the pours for drinkers who care more about maturity and structure than raw impact.

For the spice-forward camp, Thomas H. Handy is the most polarizing expression in the collection. It’s the youngest whiskey in the lineup and the only barrel-proof rye, aged around six years and bottled uncut: cinnamon, clove, black pepper, candied orange, rye bread. Collectors who primarily drink bourbon sometimes find Handy jarring; rye enthusiasts often find it the most exciting bottle in the whole release.

When does BTAC come out, and how do I actually find it?

The Buffalo Trace Antique Collection is typically released in the fall, usually September or October. But that’s the distillery announcement date, not when bottles reach shelves. Distribution timelines mean retail availability can lag by weeks, and it varies by state. The practical playbook for finding BTAC at retail is the same as any other highly allocated bourbon: build a real relationship with a specialty retailer, ask about their allocation process (not their waiting list), and show up consistently as a customer throughout the year, not just at release time. Our deeper guide on how to get on a bourbon allocation list covers that process in full.

Because BTAC moves through standard state-level alcohol channels, how bourbon state lotteries work is also directly relevant. Some states run formal lotteries for BTAC bottles rather than leaving distribution entirely to retailer discretion.

What is BTAC worth on the secondary market?

A question we hear often: should collectors pay secondary prices to complete a BTAC set?

The honest answer has shifted in 2026. With the exception of Eagle Rare, which has held steady with a GSRP of around $65, secondary prices across major allocated releases are down. Van Winkle whiskeys are off almost 9% apiece, and BTAC has followed a similar softening trend.

BTAC is released only once per year in very small quantities, which has historically made each bottle highly collectible and pushed secondary prices into the thousands. But “commanding thousands” and “worth thousands” are different claims. If your goal is to drink the whiskey, the gap between a $60 retail bottle and a $600 secondary bottle is rarely justified by what’s in the glass. If your goal is to hold and potentially resell, the current market, shaped by a broader bourbon surplus putting downward pressure on secondary premiums, deserves careful analysis before you commit. Track secondary trends using auction history data and resist FOMO-driven buying.

For collectors who own BTAC bottles, knowing their current secondary value matters for insuring a bourbon collection and for understanding how total collection value shifts year over year. A dedicated bourbon inventory app handles that more reliably than a spreadsheet.

Does BTAC ever change its lineup?

Yes, and 2025 was a significant example. For the collection’s 25th anniversary, Buffalo Trace added Colonel E.H. Taylor, honoring the man many call the father of modern bourbon. It’s a 15-year-old bourbon bottled at 100 proof, using Buffalo Trace’s classic bourbon recipe.

That addition raised a natural question among collectors: does a sixth bottle dilute what makes BTAC special, or does it deepen the collection? The answer probably depends on your philosophy. Purists who value BTAC as a fixed, five-bottle annual tradition see any change as a disruption. Collectors who appreciate the E.H. Taylor Jr. line independently welcomed it as a logical extension of the distillery’s prestige tier.

Historically, the lineup’s evolution has always been slow and deliberate. Since its launch in 2000, the Antique Collection grew from a trilogy into what we know today, suggesting Buffalo Trace treats lineup changes as rare events rather than annual marketing moves.

How should a collector approach BTAC as a cellar strategy?

BTAC sits at the intersection of two collector instincts that can pull in opposite directions: the desire to open and experience great whiskey, and the desire to hold rare bottles for their provenance and potential value. Neither impulse is wrong, but BTAC’s barrel-proof expressions are genuinely best experienced as pours, not just as shelf trophies.

A practical approach for most collectors: if you’re fortunate enough to land a retail bottle, open at least one vintage within a few years of acquisition. Log your tasting notes, color, nose, palate, finish, proof, rickhouse characteristics if disclosed, and compare them against future vintage purchases. That’s how a BTAC bottle becomes part of a bourbon library rather than just a conversation piece. Collectors who pursue vertical tastings of successive BTAC vintages often cite the year-over-year proof and flavor variation as one of the most engaging exercises in the hobby.

If you do hold bottles, store them properly: consistent cool temperature, away from light and UV, upright to protect the cork on high-proof expressions. Our guide on how to store bourbon covers the specifics that matter for long-term cellar storage.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many bottles are in the Buffalo Trace Antique Collection?

BTAC traditionally includes five expressions: George T. Stagg, William Larue Weller, Eagle Rare 17 Year Old, Thomas H. Handy Sazerac Rye, and Sazerac 18 Year Old Rye. For the 2025 anniversary release, Buffalo Trace added a sixth: Colonel E.H. Taylor 15 Year.

When does the Buffalo Trace Antique Collection come out each year?

BTAC is typically released in September or October. Exact dates vary by year and by state, and bottles often reach retail shelves weeks after the official announcement due to distribution timelines.

What proof is BTAC bottled at?

Most BTAC expressions are released at barrel proof, which changes every year. George T. Stagg and William Larue Weller are both uncut and unfiltered, so their proof fluctuates significantly. Eagle Rare 17 is bottled at 101 proof and Sazerac 18 at a consistent 90 proof, the two fixed-proof expressions in the lineup.

Is the Buffalo Trace Antique Collection worth buying at secondary market prices?

That depends on your goals. Secondary prices for BTAC have softened in recent years. Wine-Searcher data showed even Van Winkle expressions down roughly 9% in 2026, and BTAC has followed a similar trend. If you're buying to drink, retail price is the only defensible entry point. If you're collecting for value, track market trends carefully before paying a large premium.

Which BTAC bottle should a new collector start with?

Most experts point to Sazerac 18 Year Old Rye as the most approachable entry. It's bottled at 90 proof, the lowest in the lineup, and its 18 years of aging produce a restrained, layered complexity that rewards careful sipping. Eagle Rare 17 is the next step up, offering elegant oak without the heat of a barrel-proof expression.
