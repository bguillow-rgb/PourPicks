Old Fashioned: Rye or Bourbon? How to Choose

Rye or bourbon in an Old Fashioned? Bourbon makes it rounder and sweeter, rye makes it drier and spicier. Which to use, how to adjust the recipe, and what most drinkers actually reach for.

By Pour Picks · Published July 28, 2026

In brief

Both work. Bourbon makes a rounder, sweeter Old Fashioned that most people prefer as a first drink; rye makes a drier, spicier one that cuts the sugar and reads more classic. Use bourbon if you like it soft and approachable, rye if you want backbone. High-rye bourbon splits the difference.

The Old Fashioned is the one cocktail almost every whiskey drinker eventually makes at home, and the first fork in the road is which whiskey goes in it. Rye or bourbon? The honest answer is that both are correct, and the difference is real enough that you should choose on purpose rather than grabbing whatever bottle is closest.

Here is what each one does to the drink, and how to decide.

Should you use rye or bourbon in an Old Fashioned?

Use bourbon when you want the drink round, warm, and easy. Bourbon’s corn-forward sweetness (vanilla, caramel, a little honey) layers with the sugar and orange in the glass and produces a soft, crowd-pleasing cocktail. It is the version most people picture.

Use rye when you want the drink drier and more assertive. Rye’s pepper and baking-spice character pushes back against the sugar, so a rye Old Fashioned tastes less sweet and more savory on the same build. It reads as the more “classic” or old-guard version, and it is what a lot of cocktail bars default to.

Neither is more authentic than the other. The drink is older than the modern split between the two grains, and it survives whatever you pour into it. The choice is about the mood you want, not correctness.

What does bourbon bring to an Old Fashioned?

Bourbon makes the friendliest version of the drink. The sweetness that defines it (at least 51% corn in the mash bill by law) sits comfortably next to the sugar cube and the orange peel, so nothing fights. You get a cocktail that is approachable on the first sip and forgiving if your measurements are loose.

The tradeoff is that a bourbon Old Fashioned can tip sweet, especially if you use a wheated bourbon or a heavy hand with the simple syrup. If you like a dessert-adjacent drink, that is a feature. If you want the whiskey to stay in charge, dial the sugar back or reach for a higher-proof bourbon that holds its ground as the ice melts. A 100-proof pour like Old Forester 100 or Wild Turkey 101 keeps its spine better than an 80-proof bottle.

What does rye bring to an Old Fashioned?

Rye makes the drink drier, spicier, and more grown-up. The same peppery, herbal notes that make rye polarizing when sipped neat are exactly what you want against sugar and bitters: they keep the cocktail from turning into candy. A rye Old Fashioned finishes cleaner and lingers longer on the spice.

This is why so many bartenders quietly prefer rye here. It gives the drink structure. The classic move is to keep the sweetener the same as you would for bourbon and simply let the rye balance it, so the drink reads less sweet without you doing any extra math. If you have only ever had bourbon Old Fashioneds, making one with a benchmark rye like Rittenhouse or Sazerac is a genuine “oh, that’s the difference” moment.

What do most drinkers actually reach for?

For all the debate, most people default to bourbon, and it is not close. Across Pour Picks cellars, logged bourbons outnumber ryes on the shelf by roughly 20 to 1 (140 bourbons to 7 ryes among the American-whiskey bottles a few dozen early users have logged as of July 2026). Even accounting for the small, early sample, the lopsidedness is the point: the average whiskey drinker simply keeps far more bourbon on hand, so bourbon is what lands in the mixing glass most nights.

That is a statement about what is within arm’s reach, not about what is better in the drink. If anything, it argues for keeping one good rye around specifically for cocktails, since you almost certainly already have the bourbon covered.

Is high-rye bourbon the best of both?

If you cannot decide, high-rye bourbon is the honest middle path. These are still legally bourbon (majority corn, so they keep the sweetness) but carry enough rye in the mash bill to bring real spice. About a third of the bourbons in the Pour Picks catalog that list a full mash bill run 20% rye or higher (42 of 126), so this is a well-populated middle, not a rare unicorn.

Bottles like 1792 Small Batch (15% rye), Old Forester 100 (18% rye), or Four Roses’ high-rye recipes give you the round bourbon base with a spicy kick on top. In an Old Fashioned, that means a drink that is sweet enough to be easy but spicy enough to stay interesting. For a lot of home bartenders, a high-rye bourbon is the single best “only one bottle” answer for this cocktail. If you want to understand the spectrum, wheated vs high-rye bourbon and what a mash bill actually does cover the grain math behind it.

How should you adjust the recipe for each?

The build barely changes; the balance does. A standard Old Fashioned is roughly 2 oz whiskey, a scant teaspoon of sugar or a quarter-ounce of simple syrup, two to three dashes of Angostura bitters, stirred over ice with an expressed orange peel.

  • With bourbon: consider slightly less sweetener, since the whiskey is already bringing sugar-adjacent notes. If you like it sweet, leave it.
  • With rye: keep the sweetener as-is, or nudge it up a hair. The spice gives you room, and a bone-dry rye Old Fashioned can read austere to some palates.
  • With high-rye bourbon: the default recipe usually needs no adjustment at all. This is part of why it is such a reliable pick.
  • Proof matters more than grain: a 90 to 100 proof whiskey survives the dilution as your ice melts. Anything under 86 proof risks getting buried.

Glassware and ice do real work here too. A single large cube melts slower and keeps the drink from watering out, and a proper rocks glass concentrates the orange-oil aromatics right under your nose.

Final answer: which should you pour?

If you are making one drink for someone who is not a hardcore whiskey person, use bourbon. If you are making one for yourself and you want structure and a cleaner finish, use rye. If you want a single bottle that handles the cocktail well every time without fuss, buy a high-rye bourbon and stop overthinking it. The deeper you go, the more you will keep all three around, and the bourbon-vs-rye split stops being a decision and starts being a mood.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is an Old Fashioned traditionally made with rye or bourbon?

The original Old Fashioned predates the modern bourbon-vs-rye divide and was made with whatever American whiskey was on hand, which in the 1800s often meant rye, since rye dominated the northeast. Bourbon took over as rye nearly vanished mid-century, so most people today have had far more bourbon Old Fashioneds. Neither is wrong. The drink is a template, not a fixed recipe.

Does a rye Old Fashioned need less sugar?

Usually a touch less, or you can leave the sugar the same and let the rye's spice balance it. Rye is drier and more peppery than bourbon, so it stands up to the sugar and bitters without getting cloying. Many bartenders keep the sweetener identical and just enjoy that a rye version reads less sweet on the same build.

What proof whiskey is best for an Old Fashioned?

Something in the 90 to 100 proof range is the sweet spot. Below 86 proof the whiskey can get lost under the sugar and bitters; a bottled-in-bond or 100-proof pour holds its own against dilution as the ice melts. Barrel-proof whiskey works too but you will want a little extra water or a larger ice cube to open it up.

Can you use wheated bourbon in an Old Fashioned?

Yes, and it makes a soft, dessert-leaning version. Wheated bourbons like Maker's Mark or Larceny bring almost no secondary-grain spice, so the drink reads gentle and sweet. It is the opposite end of the spectrum from a rye Old Fashioned. Great for someone easing into whiskey cocktails, less interesting if you want contrast against the sugar.
