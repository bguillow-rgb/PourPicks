How to Build a Bourbon Wish List: A Collector's System for Hunting Smarter

A well-built bourbon wish list tells you exactly what to hunt next — and why. Here's how collectors organize, prioritize, and use one effectively.

By Pour Picks · Published November 9, 2026

In brief

Build a bourbon wish list by grouping bottles into tiers, unicorns to hunt opportunistically, mid-range targets to watch at retail, and everyday finds to grab on sight. Add flavor notes, price ceilings, and why each bottle matters to you. Review and prune the list every 90 days so it stays actionable.

A bourbon wish list sounds simple: just write down bottles you want. But a list without structure tends to calcify into a mix of impossible grails, forgotten mid-shelf targets, and bottles you already own. The goal isn’t length. It’s precision. It should tell you, on any given day, exactly what to look for and what to walk past.

Why do so many bourbon collectors end up with a useless want list?

A question we hear often: collectors spend months building a wish list, then find themselves ignoring it entirely at the liquor store because the list doesn’t connect to real buying decisions.

The most common failure is a flat list with no context, just bottle names and nothing else attached. When you walk into a shop and spot a bottle on that list at $20 over your mental budget, you have no information to make the call. When a store pick of something adjacent appears, you don’t know if it qualifies. A structureless wish list doesn’t save you decision fatigue; it just shifts it.

Treat your wish list like a purchasing brief, not a bucket list. Each entry should answer three questions: What is it? What’s it worth to me? Why does it belong in my cellar? When you can answer all three in under ten seconds, you can make a confident call at the shelf, or walk away without regret.

How should I tier bottles on my wish list?

The most effective collector wish lists use a three-tier system based on hunt difficulty, not on prestige:

TierLabelWhat goes hereHunt approach
1Long GameHeavily allocated or lottery-only bottles (BTAC, Pappy, William Larue Weller)Register for state lotteries and store allocation lists; check in quarterly
2Active HuntMid-range releases available at retail but inconsistently (Eagle Rare 10, Elijah Craig Barrel Proof, Four Roses Limited Edition)Regular store visits, retailer relationships, regional searches
3Grab on SightBroadly available but worth noting for a specific reason (particular batch, proof, or store pick)Immediate purchase when spotted at or near MSRP

Tier 1 bottles require patience and process. You should already be on a bourbon allocation list for the retailer or state lottery before adding anything here. Tier 3 bottles should be priced and committed to: if you need to deliberate at the shelf, they don’t belong in this tier.

Whiskey auction data from 2025-2026 shows secondary prices for major allocated releases moved significantly. Pappy Van Winkle 23 Year dropped roughly 30% from its peak, while some mid-tier allocated bottles became more accessible as broader inventory caught up. Tier 2 is getting more interesting, not less, and your active-hunt tier deserves the most attention right now.

What details should I log for each bottle?

Readers frequently ask: what’s the minimum information that actually makes a wish list entry useful?

For each bottle, capture:

  • Full name and distillery (not just the brand, the specific expression matters)
  • Proof and age statement if relevant
  • Typical MSRP and your personal price ceiling for secondary-market purchases
  • Flavor profile shorthand, one to three words based on what you’ve read or tasted in a related expression (e.g., “wheated, dried fruit, long finish”)
  • Why it belongs in your cellar, not just “it’s good” but how it fills a gap, rounds out a distillery vertical, or fits a style you’re building toward

That last field matters more than it sounds. Without it, your list reflects hype rather than your actual collection goals. If you’re already deep in wheated bourbons, adding more Weller expressions might be redundant. Your list should surface that tension, not hide it.

If you use a bourbon inventory app to track what you already own, your wish list works best as the deliberate opposite: a view of what’s missing from your cellar by style, proof range, or distillery, not just what’s popular.

How do I prioritize when everything on my list is worth buying?

This one comes up a lot. The wish list has 25 bottles on it, you have $150 to spend this month, and you’re staring at three of them on the shelf simultaneously. How do you pick?

A simple scoring approach helps. For each bottle you’re deciding between, rate it on three factors from 1-3:

  1. Availability: how often does this show up at retail? (3 = rarely, 1 = frequently)
  2. Value gap: how much does retail undercut secondary prices? (3 = huge gap, 1 = minimal)
  3. Collection fit: does it fill a genuine gap in your cellar? (3 = yes, 1 = you already have something similar)

A bottle scoring 7-9 is a confident buy. Bottles scoring 4-6 can wait for next month. This isn’t a perfect algorithm. It’s a friction-reducer that keeps emotion out of the decision. Many collectors find that just writing the scores down makes the right call obvious without doing the math.

The alternative is impulse buying based on whatever’s available, which slowly turns a considered cellar into a random assortment. Knowing how to organize a bourbon collection means knowing not just what’s on your shelf, but what belongs there next.

Should my wish list include bottles I’ve already tasted vs. ones I haven’t?

This one comes up a lot: is a wish list for bottles you’re chasing on reputation, or only bottles you’ve confirmed you enjoy?

Both have a place, but label them differently. A tasted-and-loved bottle, say a barrel pick you had at a bottle share, belongs in your Active Hunt tier with high confidence. A reputation-driven entry (you’ve read the reviews, you know the mash bill, you like the distillery’s other expressions) is a qualified candidate but deserves a note: untasted, based on X.

That distinction matters at the shelf. Spending $80-120 on an untasted bottle is a different risk calculation than restocking something you’ve already journaled. If you use Pour Picks to write tasting notes for bottles you’ve encountered at tastings or shares, those notes can directly inform which untasted entries on your wish list are genuinely likely to fit your palate, and which are just well-marketed.

How do I keep the list from growing into something unmanageable?

A well-maintained wish list gets shorter over time, not longer. The discipline is in the pruning.

Set a 90-day review cadence. At each review, ask four questions about every entry:

  1. Have I found it and passed? If a bottle crossed your path at a fair price and you didn’t pull the trigger, that’s information. Either your price ceiling is wrong or the bottle doesn’t actually excite you enough. Remove it or demote it.
  2. Has the market changed? Secondary prices for some mid-tier allocated bottles dropped meaningfully through 2025 and into 2026, bringing them closer to MSRP territory. A bottle that belonged in Tier 1 six months ago might now be a reasonable Tier 2 target.
  3. Has my cellar changed? If you’ve opened three wheated bourbons this quarter and stocked up on others, the gap you were filling may no longer exist. Reviewing what you own (easier with a dedicated bourbon cellar app) should directly inform what you hunt.
  4. Do I still want this for the right reasons? Hype cycles move fast in bourbon. A bottle generating forum buzz last fall may have been overshadowed, repriced, or simply better understood by now. If the original reason you added it was hype rather than genuine fit, remove it without guilt.

Collectors who review consistently tend to stabilize around 10-20 active entries, enough variety to stay engaged across multiple retail relationships and hunt channels, focused enough to actually make progress on what matters.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many bottles should be on a bourbon wish list?

Most experienced collectors keep 10, 20 bottles on an active wish list, enough to stay engaged across multiple hunt channels without spreading attention too thin. A list longer than 30 tends to become a fantasy inventory rather than an actionable hunting guide.

Should I put allocated bottles like Pappy on my wish list?

Yes, but tier them separately. Allocated bottles (Pappy, BTAC, William Larue Weller) belong in a 'long game' tier where you're registered for state lotteries and store lists, not actively shopping. Mixing them with everyday targets makes your list feel unachievable.

What information should I track for each bottle on my wish list?

At minimum: bottle name, distillery, proof, typical retail price, your price ceiling for the secondary market, and a one-line reason you want it (flavor profile, age statement, collector significance). That context stops you from impulse-buying bottles that no longer fit your cellar.

How often should I update my bourbon wish list?

Every 60, 90 days is the practical cadence. Secondary prices shift, releases come and go, and your palate evolves. A wish list that isn't reviewed regularly fills up with bottles you've already moved past or found at auction without updating your records.
