What Is Craft Bourbon? A Collector's Guide to the Term, the Truth, and What It Actually Means for Your Cellar

Craft bourbon has no legal definition — here's what the term actually means, how to spot genuine craft production, and what collectors should know before buying.

By Pour Picks · Published September 9, 2026

In brief

Craft bourbon has no legal definition from the TTB, any distillery can use the term. In practice, it signals a small, independently owned producer making bourbon in limited quantities, often with regional grains or unusual mash bills. Quality varies enormously, so knowing what to look for matters far more than the label itself.

“Craft bourbon” is one of the most searched and most misunderstood terms in the collecting world. The short version: it means whatever the distillery wants it to mean, because the TTB has never given it a legal definition. That regulatory silence creates real confusion for collectors trying to decide whether a $65 bottle from a local distillery deserves shelf space next to their Buffalo Trace Antique Collection. This guide cuts through the marketing and tells you what actually matters.

So what does “craft bourbon” actually mean legally?

A question we hear often: collectors spot the word “craft” on a label and assume some official standard is behind it. There isn’t one.

The Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau sets the legal floor and ceiling for what a distiller can call bourbon, and those rules determine everything from grain bill percentages to the proof at which a spirit enters the barrel. But those rules say nothing about “craft.” The TTB governs distilled spirits labeling under 27 CFR Part 5, which establishes the standards of identity for American whiskey classes. A standard of identity is not a style guide or a tradition; it is a legal definition a producer must satisfy before printing a class name on a bottle. “Craft” carries no such requirement. A 5,000-barrel-a-year producer and a 5-barrel garage operation can both stamp it on a label without any accountability.

The American Craft Spirits Association (ACSA) does maintain its own working definition. Members must be independently owned and produce fewer than 750,000 proof gallons annually. But membership is voluntary, and the term itself remains open to anyone.

How did craft bourbon become such a big deal?

The explosion in small distilleries over the past fifteen years is worth taking seriously. The ACSA has reported more than 2,300 craft distilleries operating across the United States, up from fewer than 100 in 2010, a growth rate that mirrors what happened to craft beer in the 1990s. That boom produced everything from legitimate artisan operations sourcing local grain and building regional flavor profiles to slickly branded startups buying bulk bourbon from large contract distillers and slapping a hand-drawn label on it.

Consumers are asking more questions and getting more informed. People are drinking less overall but are far more curious, and instead of asking only how old a whiskey is, they increasingly want to know how it was matured and why it tastes the way it does. That curiosity is exactly what the craft segment was built to satisfy. The challenge for collectors is separating genuine craft identity from marketing that borrows the aesthetic without the substance.

How do I tell a real craft distillery from a sourced-bourbon brand?

This one comes up a lot: the label itself is your first clue, if you know where to look.

“Distilled by” means the company actually made the whiskey. “Produced by,” “Bottled by,” or “Manufactured by” may indicate that the bourbon was sourced or purchased from another distiller and rebranded. Many brands that market themselves as craft fall into the second category, legally and legitimately, but potentially misleadingly. The DSP (Distilled Spirits Plant) number on the label can confirm where distillation actually happened. Our guide to reading a DSP number on a bourbon label walks through exactly how to look it up.

Keep in mind: sourced bourbon isn’t automatically inferior. Some of the most celebrated independent bottlers use contract-distilled stock brilliantly. The issue is transparency, knowing what you’re buying.

Does craft bourbon actually taste different?

Readers frequently ask: is there a flavor signature that separates craft from big-distillery bourbon?

Sometimes yes, sometimes no, and the reasons come down to production hardware and grain choices. Pot stills allow a wider range of volatile compounds (congeners) to pass over with the alcohol vapor, so pot distillation generally creates spirits that are richer, fuller-bodied, with more distinct character from the fermented mash, often coming off the still at a lower proof of 60-80% ABV. Many craft operations run pot stills or hybrid pot-column setups specifically to preserve that grain character.

On the grain side, smaller distilleries have led the heirloom grain revival, working with open-pollinated corn varieties, heritage rye, and malted grains that the large Kentucky operations rarely use at scale. If that kind of provenance matters to your cellar, and for many collectors it increasingly does, you’ll find more of it in the craft segment than on the BOGO shelf at your local retailer. Our deep dive on heirloom grain bourbon covers this terrain in detail.

That said, craft age is a real limitation. A bourbon aged two to three years in a small barrel, a common craft shortcut to faster maturation, will taste structurally different from a well-aged Kentucky straight. Smaller barrels extract wood flavor faster but lack the slow seasonal integration that long rickhouse aging provides. Neither is categorically better, but they’re genuinely different spirits, and it’s worth knowing which you’re pouring.

What should collectors look for when evaluating a craft bourbon?

Rather than accepting or rejecting the “craft” badge at face value, run through a short checklist:

1. Who actually distilled it? Look for “distilled by” followed by the brand’s own name and a DSP number that matches their listed address. If the language is evasive, search the DSP in the TTB’s public database.

2. What’s the mash bill and grain source? Genuine craft producers are usually proud to share this. A distillery that can’t or won’t tell you their mash bill is either sourcing or has nothing distinctive to say.

3. What’s the age and barrel size? Standard 53-gallon barrels are industry norm. Small barrels (5, 10, 15 gallons) produce faster but different maturation. A 4-year whiskey from a standard barrel tells you more about age potential than a 2-year from a 5-gallon cask.

4. Is the distillery independently owned? Acquisition by a large spirits conglomerate doesn’t automatically change quality, but it complicates the “craft” narrative. Several well-known “craft” brands have been absorbed by multinationals while retaining their indie branding.

5. Is there a track record? Newer distilleries releasing their first aged stock in 2024-2026 are genuinely interesting, but also unproven. Look for production transparency and honest communication about what’s in the bottle. If you’re cataloging your collection’s value, noting which bottles represent early releases from emerging distilleries helps you track how a brand’s profile evolves over time.

Is craft bourbon worth collecting or just a marketing term to ignore?

Both, depending on the bottle. Whiskey market trends in 2026 are clearly shifting, leaving distillers and brands confronting real questions about younger drinkers, the role of limited-edition bottlings and collector mindset, and whether new products and styles still have a place in an increasingly crowded marketplace. Craft bourbon sits squarely at the center of that tension.

The strongest case for collecting craft bourbon: genuine single-barrel releases from small, transparent, grain-forward operations are among the most distinctive bottles you can own. Because production volumes are small, they rarely appear on secondary markets and often have legitimate scarcity, not manufactured allocation theater, but actual limited supply. A barrel pick from a well-regarded regional distillery, like a store pick or private barrel program release, can represent real cellar value precisely because it’s irreplaceable.

The case for skepticism is just as strong. “Craft” has been attached to enough sourced, underage, or simply mediocre bourbon that collectors have been burned often enough to treat it as a red flag until proven otherwise. There’s been a shift from consumers strictly caring about age statements and proof to more interest in flavor nuance and production, and the savvy move is to apply that same curiosity to craft claims before money changes hands.

The working rule for your cellar: evaluate the distillery, not the marketing badge. A few hours of research on who actually made the bourbon, how long it was aged, and what the mash bill looks like will tell you far more than “craft” ever will.

How does craft bourbon fit into a larger collection?

A question we hear often: should craft bottles live alongside allocated Kentucky releases, or are they a separate category entirely?

The best-run cellars treat craft as its own segment, a way to support regional producers, explore non-standard flavor profiles, and own genuinely limited bottles that no auction house will ever list. If you organize your collection by production style or region, a dedicated craft section makes both practical and conceptual sense.

When valuing your collection for insurance or resale purposes, craft bottles require more research than allocated staples. There’s often no robust secondary market price history. Apps like Pour Picks let you log production notes, distillery details, and purchase price alongside your other bottles, so the provenance research you did at acquisition is still attached to the bottle five years later when you’re deciding whether to open it or trade it.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a legal definition of craft bourbon? No. The TTB’s standards of identity for bourbon make no distinction between craft and non-craft production. Any distillery, large or small, can legally print “craft” on a label without meeting any specific requirement.

How is craft bourbon different from regular bourbon? The difference is usually scale and ownership: craft distilleries are smaller, often independently owned, and may use locally sourced grains, open-top fermenters, or pot stills. But since the term is unregulated, some “craft” brands are actually sourced from large contract distillers like MGP.

Can a craft distillery make sourced bourbon? Yes, and many do, especially in their early years before their own aged stock is ready. A brand can legally call itself a craft distillery while bottling bourbon distilled elsewhere. Look for “distilled by” vs. “produced by” on the label to tell the difference.

How many craft distilleries are in the United States? The American Craft Spirits Association has reported more than 2,300 craft distilleries operating in the U.S., up from fewer than 100 in 2010. Not all produce bourbon, but the segment has grown dramatically over the past fifteen years.

Is craft bourbon worth collecting? Some craft bourbons are genuinely distinctive and collectible, especially single-barrel releases, limited runs using heirloom grains, or bottles from distilleries with a strong regional identity. Others are undistinguished or simply sourced stock in premium packaging. Research the producer before you buy.

What does “distilled by” vs. “produced by” mean on a craft label? “Distilled by” means that named company actually made the whiskey. “Produced by,” “bottled by,” or “manufactured by” are softer terms that often indicate the liquid came from a different distillery. For any bottle claiming craft credentials, “distilled by” followed by the brand’s own name is the standard you want to see.
