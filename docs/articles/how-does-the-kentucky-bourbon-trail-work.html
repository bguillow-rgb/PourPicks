How Does the Kentucky Bourbon Trail Work?

The Kentucky Bourbon Trail now covers 68+ experiences across two tracks. This explains how the trails work, what replaced the paper passport, and how to plan a smarter visit.

By Pour Picks · Published September 19, 2026

In brief

The Kentucky Bourbon Trail is a Kentucky Distillers' Association program spanning two tracks, the main Trail and the Craft Tour, with 68+ total distillery experiences across Kentucky. The physical passport retired in mid-2025 and was replaced by a free digital trip builder. Collectors use both tracks to source bottles, attend barrel picks, and build firsthand cellar knowledge.

The Kentucky Bourbon Trail is one of those things bourbon collectors talk about constantly, but the details get fuzzy fast. Which trail is which? What happened to the passport? Is it worth doing more than once? If you’ve been meaning to sort it all out before planning a trip, this is the guide.

Okay, so what actually is the Kentucky Bourbon Trail, and who runs it?

The Kentucky Bourbon Trail is a program sponsored by the Kentucky Distillers’ Association (KDA) to promote the bourbon whiskey industry in Kentucky. Think of it as a road-trip framework rather than a single attraction. The KDA has registered “Kentucky Bourbon Trail” as a protected trademark and launched the program in 1999, at the time including seven of the eight distilleries in the region.

The program has grown dramatically since then. The digital trip builder now lets visitors create shareable itineraries with drive times, dining, and lodging across 68 experiences. That’s not a typo: 68 experiences, spread across the main Trail and the Craft Tour. For a collector, that footprint is both exciting and overwhelming, which is exactly why planning matters before you go.

Not every famous Kentucky distillery participates officially. Buffalo Trace Distillery and Barton 1792 Distillery were withdrawn from the KDA at the beginning of 2010 when Sazerac purchased Barton, though Sazerac’s spokesperson stated they still consider themselves “very much a part of the Bourbon Trail” and both distilleries continue to host public tours. In practice, you can and should still visit Buffalo Trace. It just won’t appear on the official KDA map.

What’s the difference between the main Kentucky Bourbon Trail and the Craft Tour?

A question we hear often: these two tracks get conflated constantly, but they serve genuinely different collector goals.

The main Kentucky Bourbon Trail features 18 flagship distillery experiences: the large, established producers. Think Maker’s Mark, Four Roses, Woodford Reserve, Wild Turkey, Heaven Hill, and others at that scale. These are the stops with full production operations, elaborate visitor centers, and the widest selection of expressions available for purchase.

The Kentucky Bourbon Trail Craft Tour extends the program to a much broader roster of smaller, independent producers scattered across the state. With an ever-growing roster of distilleries, the program became too large to complete via a stamp book, which is precisely why the KDA restructured it and why the old passport is gone.

For collectors, the Craft Tour is where discovery happens. Smaller distilleries often carry grain-forward or experimental expressions you won’t find allocated or on the secondary. If your cellar already has the flagship names covered, the Craft Tour fills gaps that mainstream release calendars never touch.

What happened to the Kentucky Bourbon Trail passport?

This one comes up a lot: collectors who visited a few years ago remember the physical stamp book fondly and are confused when they can’t find it anymore.

The physical passport retired after July 1, 2025. The free digital “Build Your Own Bourbon Trail” trip builder at trip-builder.kybourbontrail.com replaced it. If you had an older passport, the Kentucky Bourbon Trail continued to honor passports and field guides purchased and registered before July 1, 2024, until July 1, 2025.

The change wasn’t arbitrary. The trail had grown to 46 distilleries, meaning a complete run would take more than 10 days. The KDA moved away from the stamp model to encourage visitors to slow down and spend more time at individual distilleries rather than cramming in as many stops as possible.

The tradeoff is real. For years, the printed Field Guide gave first-timers a tangible framework: a map, a checklist, and a souvenir. That structure is gone. Serious collectors who liked the completionist angle now lean on the digital trip builder and their own spreadsheets, or a cellar app, to keep distillery records organized across multiple trips.

What is the Urban Bourbon Trail, and is it worth a collector’s time?

Readers frequently ask: is the Louisville passport program just for tourists, or does it actually surface bottles worth having?

Louisville’s Urban Bourbon Trail connects more than 40 of the city’s bars and restaurants into a single passport-driven experience. Created by Louisville Tourism, it covers bars, restaurants, and hotels across Louisville, each committed to stocking a meaningful selection. Every participating establishment carries a minimum of 50 different bourbons and trains its staff to guide guests through them.

For a collector, that floor matters. You’re not landing in a bar with four bourbons and a bartender who doesn’t know what a mash bill is. The Urban Trail is how you discover your next bottle in the wild, not on a website, in the hands of someone who pours it daily. Drink a few things, ask questions, and let the pour drive your next cellar addition.

Once you’ve collected the required number of stamps, redeem your completed passport at the Louisville Visitor Center for a completion prize. The required number and the prize vary by year.

When should I go, and how do I book?

The biggest mistake first-time trail visitors make is arriving on a Saturday in fall without a reservation. A Tuesday morning at Buffalo Trace is a different experience entirely: small groups, chatty guides, unhurried tastings, and the feeling that you’ve found something rather than joined a procession. If at all possible, overlap your trip with weekdays. Save Saturday for Bardstown’s downtown.

Buffalo Trace’s Hard Hat Tour should be booked three or more weeks in advance. Most flagship distilleries, including Woodford Reserve, Four Roses, and Maker’s Mark, fill up weeks out during peak season (September through November, when fall releases overlap with peak tourism). Book everything before you leave home.

With only one full day, you’ll get more from two or three immersive distillery visits than from trying to stamp-collect across many stops. That holds across multi-day trips too: two deep visits beat five rushed ones, especially if you want to actually talk production with the people who make the juice.

Does the trail actually help collectors find bottles?

This is the question that matters most, and the honest answer is yes, but not in the way you might expect.

Distillery gift shops are not the secondary market. You won’t walk in and reliably find allocated bottles at MSRP on a Tuesday in October. What you will find are distillery-exclusive expressions, gift shop-only bottlings, and private barrel selections that never reach retail shelves outside Kentucky. Access to exclusive product hasn’t disappeared with the passport. It’s now built into the distillery experience itself, particularly at gift shops tied to barrel-pick programs.

The deeper collector value is the firsthand production knowledge you accumulate across multiple visits. Understanding how a rickhouse sits on a given campus, how a distillery actually runs its barrel selection for store picks, or what a private barrel program looks like on the ground: that’s information you cannot get from a spec sheet. The trail is field research for your cellar, not a shopping trip.

Once you’re home, everything you tasted, toured, and sourced deserves a home in your collection records. That’s where keeping your cellar organized pays off, especially if you’re cross-referencing distillery-exclusive bottles against their estimated collection value over time.

How is the 2026 season different from prior years?

The broader spirits industry is in a different place than it was two or three years ago. Distillers are confronting real questions about younger drinkers, the role of limited-edition bottlings, and whether new products still have room in a crowded marketplace.

That context matters practically for trail visitors. In 2026, inflation is cooling, retailers are holding more inventory, and limited releases no longer sell out instantly. The frantic energy of peak bourbon-boom years has softened. Distillery gift shops have more stock. Tour wait times at some producers have eased. For a collector who postponed a trail trip because of crowds and scarcity, 2026 may be the most approachable year to go in nearly a decade.

James B. Beam Distilling Co. in Clermont, KY, for example, offers a 75-minute grain-to-glass tour and tasting with five pours, hands-on barrel work, and rare-bottle access, all on one campus, and experiences like that are easier to book right now than they were at the height of the bourbon boom.
