What Is a Non-Distiller Producer (NDP) Bourbon? How to Spot One, Read the Label, and Decide If It Matters

An NDP buys finished whiskey from another distillery and bottles it under its own brand. Learn how to spot one on the label, why it matters for collectors, and when it doesn't.

By Pour Picks · Published October 12, 2026

In brief

A Non-Distiller Producer (NDP) is a brand that purchases bulk whiskey from another distillery, then bottles and markets it under its own name. The liquid is legally bourbon if it meets federal standards, but the brand on the label did not distill it. NDPs range from fully transparent blenders to brands that obscure their source entirely.

When you start reading bourbon labels carefully, really reading them, you notice that “Distilled by” and “Produced by” don’t always point to the same company. That gap is where Non-Distiller Producers live. Understanding NDPs is one of the more clarifying moments in a collector’s education: it reframes how you read a label, how you evaluate a brand’s story, and how you decide what a bottle is actually worth to your bourbon cellar.

So what exactly is an NDP, and why does the bourbon world have them?

NDP stands for non-distiller producer. An NDP brand purchases whiskey from a separate distillery and bottles it under its own name. The brand on the front may have a beautifully crafted origin story, a grandfather’s recipe, a historic county, a hand-drawn map, but the actual distillation happened somewhere else, usually at a large commercial facility.

This isn’t a modern workaround. For much of the 19th and early 20th centuries, Kentucky distillers routinely sold bulk whiskey to grocers and other producers, who then sold it to their customers. The bourbon boom of the 2000s and 2010s brought a wave of new brands to market before their own distilleries were operational, which made the NDP question more visible to collectors than it had ever been before.

That boom also brought opacity. Many producers were cagey about where their whiskey actually came from, and that’s what turned “NDP” into a loaded term in collector circles. Not the practice itself, but the marketing that sometimes obscured it.

How do I find the NDP disclosure on an actual bottle label?

A question we hear often: the answer is in the fine print, usually near the UPC or on the back label near the government warning.

Federal rules require NDPs to note on the bottle that they are not distilling their own whiskey. Look for a small line that reads something like “Distilled in [State], Produced in [State].” That phrasing is saying plainly that the brand name on the label did not distill the whiskey inside.

The key distinction is between “Distilled by” and “Produced by.” A true distillery will say “Distilled by [Name] at [Location].” An NDP will often say “Produced by,” “Bottled by,” or “Selected and Bottled by.” If the distilling state in the fine print differs from the brand’s home state or mailing address, that’s your tell. A Kentucky brand whose label quietly reads “Distilled in Indiana” sourced that liquid from another producer, most likely MGP, which we cover in depth in our MGP bourbon explainer.

The DSP number is another tool. Cross-reference the DSP number on the label with TTB records and you can identify the registered distillery premises, and therefore whether the brand actually operates a still.

What’s the difference between an NDP, sourced bourbon, and an independent bottler?

This one comes up a lot: the terms overlap but they aren’t identical.

On the surface, a non-distiller producer sounds much like an independent bottler. Both share the same defining feature: neither distills its own whiskey. The meaningful difference is cultural and often commercial. The independent bottler tradition, rooted in Scotch whisky, tends to celebrate the source distillery by name. Blended Scotch accounts for around 90% of the overall Scotch market, so sourcing is simply the norm there. American NDPs, by contrast, historically built their branding around their own label identity rather than their supplier’s name.

Contract distillation is worth separating out too. A brand can commission a distillery to produce a particular mash bill, proof, or production specification. That differs from shopping through existing barrels, though the contractor still did the distilling.

For collectors, the practical question is whether the brand discloses its source. If yes, treat it like an independent bottler and evaluate the barrel selection. If no, you’re buying based on what’s in the glass alone, which is actually a fair way to assess any bottle.

Does being an NDP make a bourbon worse for collecting purposes?

Readers frequently ask: this is the question with the most nuanced answer.

In collector circles, NDP functions as both a straightforward descriptor and, sometimes, a quiet insult. Some purists won’t touch anything that didn’t come out of the brand’s own still. But the continued popularity of NDP operations like Barrell Craft Spirits, Penelope Bourbon, and O.K.I. makes that position hard to sustain on quality grounds alone.

From a provenance standpoint, NDP bottles do carry more uncertainty. You may not be able to verify the distillery, the distillation date, or the original barrel specs, and those details matter if you’re grading bottle condition or preparing a collection for insurance purposes. That said, some of the most interesting bottles on the market right now come from skilled blenders and finishers who buy rather than distill. Old Carter and Dark Arts are two examples of operations that have built real reputations through barrel selection and finishing, not through owning a still.

The secondary market has worked this out. The biggest assumption consumers make about NDP bourbon is that they’re overpaying for whiskey they could buy straight from the source distillery. In practice, a well-selected NDP barrel can outperform the source distillery’s own shelf release. The selector’s palate and the specific barrel matter more than whose name is above the door. Whiskey auction data shows that the most valuable NDP bottles tend to come from operations with verifiable sourcing history and a documented track record of consistent barrel selection, not simply from whoever tells the best story on the back label.

If you’re tracking your collection’s value over time, note the NDP status in your records. It can affect resale appetite and auction estimates for the same reason provenance matters in any collectible category.

How do transparent NDPs differ from opaque ones, and how do I tell which I’m dealing with?

Barrell Craft Spirits has always been open about sourcing from multiple distilleries. Their emphasis is on what they can create by bringing those disparate barrels together, and that transparency is a feature, not a liability. It invites evaluation on the blender’s merits.

Opaque NDPs present themselves as craft distilleries without operating a still. The tell is usually a recently founded brand with aged whiskey in the bottle, no “Distilled by” language, and a “Produced in” state that doesn’t match the distillation state in the fine print. Some of those bottles are genuinely good. Others are capitalizing on the gap between label and liquid.

A practical checklist for evaluating an unfamiliar brand:

  1. Find “Distilled in” on the label. If it names a state different from the brand’s home state, ask who distills there.
  2. Look up the DSP number in TTB’s public records to see whose premises are registered.
  3. Check the age statement. A brand founded three years ago with a 10-year-old bourbon sourced that whiskey. The math is unavoidable.
  4. Read the “produced by” vs. “distilled by” language carefully. They are legally distinct claims.
  5. Search the brand against known bulk suppliers. MGP Indiana, Bardstown Bourbon Company, and Castle & Key are among the major contract producers whose whiskeys appear under many labels.

What should a collector actually do with this information?

The NDP question rarely produces a simple “buy” or “avoid” answer. If you value whiskey primarily by where it’s from or how old it is, you may find better deals buying direct from the distillery. If you care more about what’s in the glass, plenty of NDP selections hold up.

The more useful framing: treat source transparency as one data point in your overall assessment, not a disqualifier. An NDP that clearly states “Sourced from Indiana, finished in Cognac casks, batch number 22” gives you more to evaluate than a house distillery with no age statement and no barrel data. Transparency is the variable worth rewarding, regardless of which type of producer is offering it. When you’re building out a bourbon rotation or stocking a reference library, a mix of distillery-made and well-selected NDP bottles often produces more interesting side-by-sides than sticking to one category.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does NDP stand for in bourbon?

NDP stands for Non-Distiller Producer, a company that sources finished or aging whiskey from a separate distillery rather than operating its own still, then bottles and markets it under its own brand name.

How can you tell if a bourbon is made by an NDP from the label?

Look for two different state names in the fine print: 'Distilled in [State X], Bottled in [State Y]' signals a different distillery made the liquid. If the distilling state differs from the bottler's address, the brand is almost certainly an NDP. Also watch for 'Produced by' versus 'Distilled by', they are not the same claim.

Is NDP bourbon lower quality than distillery-made bourbon?

Not necessarily. Quality depends on barrel selection, blending skill, and finishing, not whether the brand owns a still. Highly regarded bottlers like Barrell Craft Spirits and Old Carter operate as NDPs and produce critically acclaimed releases.

What is the difference between an NDP and an independent bottler?

The terms overlap but have different roots. 'Independent bottler' is a Scotch whisky tradition where companies bottle single-cask or vatted whiskies from named distilleries, often disclosing the source. American NDPs more commonly sell under their own brand without disclosing the source distillery, though some are now more transparent.

Are NDPs required to disclose their source distillery on the label?

Federal law requires that if the bottling state differs from the distilling state, both must appear on the label. However, NDPs are not required to name the specific source distillery, only the state. Many collectors cross-reference the DSP number or 'Distilled in' field to identify the actual producer.
