Bourbon Age Statement vs NAS: Does the Number Matter?

Short answer

A bourbon age statement tells you the minimum years the youngest whiskey in the bottle spent in oak. No Age Statement (NAS) bottles skip that number, but if labeled 'straight bourbon,' the youngest spirit inside is at least 4 years old by law. Age is one quality signal, not the only one.

Pick up a bottle of Eagle Rare 10 next to a bottle of Buffalo Trace with no number on the label and the question is fair: does that “10” mean the Eagle Rare is better? Does the missing number on Buffalo Trace mean something is being hidden? The answer depends on what you’re actually measuring.

What does a bourbon age statement actually tell you?

A question we hear often: collectors see two similar bottles at similar prices, one with an age and one without, and want to know what they’re actually comparing.

The age statement tells you how long the youngest whiskey in the bottle spent in oak barrels, not the average, and not the oldest component. A 10-year age statement on a blended bourbon could mean most of the liquid is 13 or 14 years old, with a small portion of 10-year barrels pulling the stated age down. Even when the label says 12 years old, there’s often older whiskey in the bottle.

If the label says “straight bourbon,” the youngest whiskey inside is at least 4 years old. That’s the TTB minimum for the “straight” designation without an age statement. An age statement is a floor guarantee, not a full picture of what’s in the bottle.

What does “No Age Statement” actually mean? Is the bourbon young?

Most bourbon bottles don’t carry an age statement. Buffalo Trace, Maker’s Mark, Woodford Reserve, Four Roses Small Batch, Wild Turkey 101, all NAS. That doesn’t mean the whiskey is young. It means the distiller chose not to commit to a specific number on the label.

NAS whiskey is a blend of variously aged whiskeys, selected and combined by the distiller to hit a consistent flavor target. That blending freedom is the core argument for NAS: a master distiller can pull the best-performing barrels across multiple age cohorts rather than being limited to whatever happened to be ready at exactly year eight or twelve.

Blanton’s Original, Four Roses Small Batch, Basil Hayden’s, and Woodford Reserve Distiller’s Select have never carried an age statement. All are considered benchmark bourbons by critics and collectors.

Why do distilleries drop age statements from beloved bottles?

Readers frequently ask: why would a brand remove an age statement that collectors trusted and used as a buying signal?

The use or non-use of an age statement is a marketing decision. When demand outgrows the supply of barrels at a specific age, the age statement becomes a production ceiling. If you have an age-stated product and more demand than inventory can support, you have three choices: keep the age statement and allocate the brand, keeping sales flat; keep the age statement and raise the price enough to grow profit despite flat volume; or drop the statement, which gives the most blending flexibility.

The most-cited example is Elijah Craig. Heaven Hill dropped the “12 Year” age statement in 2016, but the stock still skews heavily into the 8-12-year range. It remains one of the best sub-$35 bourbons on the shelf. Collector reaction was skeptical at first, but the liquid quality held and the brand is still highly regarded.

Higher age statements are often used to position special releases as premium offerings, which gives distilleries a real incentive to keep or introduce age statements on higher-priced expressions even when those statements are more marketing signal than quality guarantee.

Is older bourbon actually better? What’s the sweet spot?

Older bourbon is not automatically better bourbon. The sweet spot for most bourbon sits between 6 and 12 years, where oak has contributed vanilla, caramel, and baking spice without overwhelming the grain character.

Beyond that window, Kentucky’s climate works against extended aging. Hot summers and cold winters cause rapid expansion and contraction in charred oak barrels, which accelerates extraction. This is part of why bourbon matures faster than Scotch whisky, and why bourbons aged 15 or more years can tip into astringency if they weren’t positioned on cooler, lower warehouse floors from the start. New charred barrels and Kentucky’s temperature swings both push maturation faster.

A 6-year-old barrel can taste dramatically different from another 6-year-old depending on warehouse position, char level, and climate. Two bottles with identical age statements from the same distillery can taste meaningfully different for exactly this reason.

Age statements and secondary market value: do they move prices?

This one comes up a lot: collectors want to know whether buying age-stated bourbon is a smarter investment than buying NAS.

It depends on the bottle, not the presence or absence of a number.

In 2026, the secondary market favors bottles where cornerstone releases like Pappy Van Winkle, William Larue Weller, and George T. Stagg maintain strong demand while mid-tier limited releases see significant volatility. George T. Stagg, consistently among the most valuable bottles on the secondary market, carries no age statement.

Buyers are shifting from hype to intention. Pappy Van Winkle 15, 20, and 23 Year, George T. Stagg, and William Larue Weller are now considered blue-chip collectibles. Some are age-stated; some aren’t. What they share is provenance, scarcity, and track record.

Many enthusiasts have shifted toward bottles that balance flavor, maturity, and value over hype, bourbons that deliver serious quality at a justifiable price rather than pure scarcity.

Does the 2026 market shift change how collectors should think about NAS?

Consumers are less focused on age statements and proof than they used to be, and more interested in flavor and production. “People are drinking less overall, but they’re far more curious,” noted one industry observer. “Instead of asking only how old a whiskey is, they’re increasingly interested in how it was matured and why it tastes the way it does.”

Consumers want real reasons for price jumps: age statements, provenance, and meaningful craft credentials, not prestige alone. A well-sourced NAS bourbon from a respected distillery with a disclosed mash bill and known warehouse practices now competes credibly with mid-tier age-stated bottles on both the shelf and the secondary market.

Small-batch, single-barrel, and craft distiller bourbons are expected to gain ground as collectors prioritize taste and uniqueness. Many of those releases will be NAS.

Don’t use the presence or absence of an age statement as a buying shortcut. Use it as one data point alongside mash bill, distillery reputation, proof, and, for collection-worthy bottles, secondary market history.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does 'no age statement' mean on a bourbon bottle?

NAS means the distillery chose not to print a specific age on the label. For straight bourbon, federal law still guarantees the youngest whiskey inside spent at least 4 years in a new charred oak barrel. The absence of a number reflects a blending decision, not a quality shortcut.

Does an age statement guarantee better bourbon?

No. Age is one variable among many, mash bill, warehouse location, entry proof, and char level all shape flavor. Many highly rated bourbons carry no age statement at all. Buffalo Trace, Maker's Mark, Woodford Reserve, and Four Roses Small Batch are all NAS and consistently score above 90 points.

What is the 'sweet spot' age range for bourbon?

Most bourbon experts and critics point to 6-12 years as the range where oak contributes vanilla, caramel, and baking spice without drowning the grain character. Beyond 12-15 years, over-oaking can make bourbon taste astringent and dry, especially in Kentucky's climate.

Do age-stated bourbons hold more secondary market value?

Generally yes for iconic expressions, age-stated releases like Eagle Rare 10 or Pappy 15/20/23 command consistent premiums. But NAS bottles with strong reputations (e.g., George T. Stagg, which is NAS) can trade well above retail too. The collector market rewards scarcity and reputation, not just the number on the label.

Why do distilleries drop age statements from popular bourbons?

Dropping an age statement gives the master distiller freedom to blend across barrel ages to maintain consistent flavor as demand grows. It also prevents a bottleneck: if demand exceeds the supply of, say, 12-year barrels, an age statement becomes a production ceiling. The tradeoff is reduced label transparency.
