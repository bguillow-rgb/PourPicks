How to Host a Bourbon Tasting at Home: Setup, Pours, Order, and Everything Else You Need

By Pour Picks · Published November 24, 2026

On this page

Short answer

To host a bourbon tasting at home, choose 3-5 bottles built around one theme, pour ½-ounce samples in Glencairn or tulip glasses, taste low-to-high proof, serve still water and plain crackers between pours, and give guests a simple note sheet. A three-pour flight totals roughly one standard drink per person.

Hosting a structured bourbon tasting at home doesn’t require a tasting room, a master distiller, or a $500 flight. It requires a plan. Pour size, bottle order, glassware, food, and pacing all shape whether guests walk away having actually learned something or just having had a good time. This guide covers both.

”How many bottles do I actually need for a good tasting?”

A question we hear often:

Three to five bottles is the working range most experienced hosts land on. A bourbon flight is a selection of small pours, typically three to five different bourbons, served side by side so you can compare them in a single sitting. Three is a genuinely good starting point: it creates immediate comparison without overwhelming anyone’s palate or memory. Five is a confident upper limit for most home settings. Push past five and guests struggle to recall what they tasted in position one by the time they reach position five, which defeats the whole point.

The other thing three-to-five solves is logistics. A standard 750 mL bottle yields roughly 25 half-ounce tasting pours, so three bottles cover a flight for up to eight guests with room to revisit. You won’t drain anything.

”Should I build the flight around a theme, or just grab whatever’s good?”

A theme is what turns a pour-and-sip session into something people actually remember. A bourbon flight is usually built around one variable, like a single distillery, a range of mash bills, or a climb in proof, and that focus is what makes the comparison land.

Here are the four themes that work best at home:

ThemeWhat it teachesExample lineup
Proof ladderHow alcohol level changes texture and perception90-proof → 100-proof BiB → 115-proof → barrel strength
Mash bill side-by-sideHow grain recipe drives flavorHigh-corn wheated vs. high-rye vs. four-grain
Distillery verticalHow one house style varies across expressionsMaker’s Mark, Maker’s 46, Maker’s Cask Strength
Age statement progressionHow barrel time changes character4-year, 8-year, 12-year from one distillery

Each of these gives guests a single variable to focus on. When every bottle is different in every way, different distillery, different age, different proof, different grain, the comparison becomes noise. Pick one variable and let the bottles answer one question. If you’ve been using Pour Picks to catalog your cellar, sorting your inventory by proof or mash bill makes pulling a thematic lineup almost effortless.

”What’s the right pour size, and does it really matter?”

Readers frequently ask:

It matters more than most hosts realize. Keep pours small. Half an ounce per glass does the job. Tasting is about attention, not volume, and small pours keep your palate sharp from first sip to last.

The math is worth knowing before you start: a full three-bourbon flight at half an ounce each is 1.5 ounces per person, and one 750 mL bottle of each bourbon covers up to eight guests with plenty left over. For barrel-proof or hazmat-strength pours (anything above 125 proof), drop to a quarter-ounce. The goal isn’t to under-serve anyone. It’s to keep everyone’s palate functional through the last pour.

One more thing worth passing along: high-proof bourbon dulls your palate, so tasting a barrel-proof pour first makes everything after it seem thin. Save the big bottles for the end of the flight.

”What’s the right order to serve the bottles?”

A flight gives you several small pours lined up so you can taste them back to back. Move from lighter, lower-proof pours toward bigger, higher-proof ones. The bold whiskeys shouldn’t come early.

Within the same proof tier, let mash bill and character guide the sequence. A delicate, high-corn wheated bourbon should come before a heavily oaked, high-rye expression at similar proof. If you’ve included any cask-finished bottles, port, sherry, or tequila finishes, place those toward the end of the flight. The residual sweetness or exotic wood character can linger and color subsequent noses.

A useful reminder for hosts: run the flight like a calm, repeatable routine. Nose everything first before you sip (your nose adjusts fast), take some notes, then take at least two sips per pour. The first sip wakes up your palate; the second gives you actual information.

”What do I actually need on the table?”

This one comes up a lot:

The list is shorter than you think. What genuinely matters, ranked by impact:

Glassware is the single biggest upgrade you can make. A Glencairn or tulip-shaped nosing glass (the tapered rim concentrates aroma in a way a wide rocks glass can’t) is worth tracking down before the night. You don’t need eight matching Glencairns; pairs that get rinsed and dried between rounds work fine for a small group. If Glencairns aren’t an option, a small white-wine glass or copita is a solid substitute. See the full breakdown in our guide to choosing the best glass for bourbon.

Still water between pours is mandatory. Give your palate 30 seconds between pours to recover. Sparkling water is a contested choice since the carbonation can irritate the palate before a high-proof pour.

Plain crackers or unsalted bread are the best palate reset. Keep them plain. Strongly flavored food mid-flight competes with the whiskey. Save cheese boards and charcuterie for after the formal tasting.

A tasting note sheet, a simple printed grid with four columns (Nose / Palate / Finish / Score), placed at each seat before guests arrive, keeps everyone engaged. If you’re running a blind tasting, label columns A, B, C instead of naming the bottles. For hosts who want to keep those notes after the night, logging them into a bourbon tasting journal or directly into Pour Picks means you’re building a reference you can actually search later.

A dump vessel matters too. Not everyone will want to finish every pour, especially at high proof. A short carafe or rocks glass at each setting signals that it’s acceptable to dump, which keeps everyone more engaged.

”How long should the whole thing take?”

A bourbon tasting takes about two hours, including arrival, food, the flight, the reveal, and conversation. That maps out roughly like this: 20 minutes of arrival and table-setting chat, 45-60 minutes for the guided flight with discussion between pours, then 30-40 minutes of open conversation, revisiting favorites, and food. Move through three to five pours, lowest to highest proof, allowing 8-12 minutes per pour with conversation.

Don’t rush the conversation between pours. That’s where the learning happens. The comparison discussions (“does this one smell sweeter to you, or is that just the proof?”) do more for building a collective palate than any score sheet.

”How do I make sure everyone gets home safely?”

This is the host’s clearest responsibility. A three-pour flight of half-ounce pours adds up to a little more than one standard drink. That’s responsible by design, but only if post-flight pours are managed with the same care. Decide in advance whether you’re offering further pours after the formal flight ends, and if so, keep them to a half-ounce as well. Have the number for a rideshare service written somewhere visible.

”What makes a home tasting actually worth repeating?”

Readers frequently ask:

One variable, one theme, one honest note sheet, and a host who keeps the pacing unhurried. The difference between a tasting people want to repeat and one that felt like homework is usually pacing and participation. Go slow enough that people can form opinions, and structure the discussion so quieter guests can contribute.

A flight is a lineup of small pours tasted side by side. When you taste one bourbon by itself, your brain doesn’t have much to work with. The comparison is what creates the memory. Build the flight around one question, let the bottles answer it, and let the conversation do the rest.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many bourbons should you have at a home tasting?

Three to five. Three bottles give clear comparisons without palate fatigue; five is a confident upper limit for most home settings. More than five makes it hard to remember what you tasted first by the end of the flight.

How much bourbon do you pour per person at a tasting?

About ½ oz (15 mL) per pour. A three-pour flight at that size totals roughly 1.5 oz per person, close to one standard drink, which keeps palates sharp. For high-proof or barrel-strength bottles, drop to ¼ oz.

What is the best order to taste bourbon in a flight?

Always pour low to high proof. Starting on a barrel-strength pour deadens the palate and makes everything that follows taste thin. Within the same proof range, move from lighter, more delicate mash bills to richer, oaked, or finished expressions.

What glass should you use for a home bourbon tasting?

A Glencairn or similar tulip-shaped nosing glass is best, the tapered rim concentrates aroma better than a rocks glass. If you don't have enough Glencairns, any small white-wine glass or copita works. Avoid wide-mouthed rocks glasses for nosing during a flight.

What food should you serve at a bourbon tasting?

Plain water crackers, unsalted bread, or mild aged cheese work best between pours to reset the palate. Avoid blue cheese, spicy food, or strong vinaigrettes mid-flight, they distort what you taste next. Save bold food pairings for after the formal flight.

How do you keep track of tasting notes at a home bourbon tasting?

Print or write a simple grid with columns for nose, palate, finish, and score. Keep note sheets at each place before guests arrive. For ongoing personal records, an app like Pour Picks lets you log tasting notes by bottle and reference them later.
