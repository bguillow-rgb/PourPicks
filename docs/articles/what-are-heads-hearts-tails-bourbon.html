What Are Heads, Hearts, and Tails in Bourbon? A Collector's Guide to Distillation Cuts

Heads, hearts, and tails are the three fractions of a bourbon distillation run. The hearts are what ends up in your bottle — here's why the cut matters for flavor.

By Pour Picks · Published September 3, 2026

In brief

Heads, hearts, and tails are the three fractions collected during bourbon distillation. The heads come off first and carry harsh, solvent-like compounds; the hearts are the clean, flavorful middle cut that goes into barrels; the tails come last and are oily and bitter. Where a distiller draws those cuts shapes the character of every bottle in your cellar.

When you crack open a bottle of bourbon, you’re drinking the distiller’s most consequential decision: exactly where to cut. Every distillation run produces three distinct fractions, heads, hearts, and tails, and only the middle one ends up in your barrel. Understanding these cuts won’t just make you a more informed collector; it will change the way you interpret what’s in the glass.

What exactly are heads, hearts, and tails, and why should I care?

The short version: these three stages are known in the U.S. as the heads, hearts, and tails, and in Scotland as foreshots, hearts, and feints. Every distillation run produces all three in sequence, and a distiller makes “cuts” during a production run to separate them. The unwanted fractions arrive at two points: the beginning and the end of the run.

The reason collectors should care is simple. The cut points are a creative decision, not just a safety procedure. Where a master distiller draws the line between heads and hearts, and between hearts and tails, shapes every flavor note on your bourbon flavor wheel. Two distilleries can use identical mashbills, the same new charred oak barrels, and the same rickhouse, and still produce dramatically different whiskeys because they cut differently. This is one of the least-discussed production variables in bourbon, yet it’s as influential as barrel char level or entry proof.

How does distillation actually work before the cuts happen?

A question we hear often: collectors read about cuts but aren’t always sure what’s happening in the still that makes them necessary in the first place.

Most bourbon is produced in two passes. The fermented grain mash (the “distiller’s beer”) goes through a column still first, which strips the alcohol into a concentrated intermediate spirit called low wine. That low wine then passes through a doubler or thumper, a pot still-style vessel, for a second distillation. It’s during this finishing distillation that a distiller makes cuts to separate the good spirit from the undesirable components the pot still creates along the way.

The chemistry driving the sequence comes down to boiling points. Low-boiling compounds enter vapor more easily, so they’re overrepresented at the start of the finishing run. Those are the heads. After they’re depleted, ethanol dominates, and that’s your hearts. As ethanol declines, heavier, high-boiling compounds become more prevalent, and you’re into the tails. The distiller monitors temperature, ABV, and sensory cues, mostly taste and smell, then physically redirects the flow of condensed spirit from one collection vessel to another at each transition point.

What’s actually in the heads, and why are they discarded?

The heads contain volatiles like acetone and smell like nail polish remover or orchard fruit. Methanol is present too, and since it’s poisonous, it has to go. More precisely, the chemical compounds with the lowest boiling points come off the still first: acetaldehyde (green, grassy flavors), acetone (nail polish remover), esters (fruity and floral), methanol, and ethanol. All of that together is what makes up the heads, also known as foreshots.

Most of the heads are discarded, but the decision isn’t always binary. The heads run oily at first, which you can feel on your fingertips and see as cloudiness in the spirit. That portion usually goes down the drain. But some distillate near the tail end of this cut may be saved and blended into the hearts. That blending of late-heads with early hearts is one of the places where a distillery’s house character starts to take shape.

What is the hearts cut, and how does it affect what I’m tasting?

This one comes up a lot: collectors who have toured distilleries hear “hearts” used almost reverentially, but the why isn’t always explained.

The hearts, sometimes called the body, come off between the heads and tails and are the most desired part of the distillate. Chemically, the purest portion is typically produced at a still temperature around 185°F to 200°F, compared to roughly 170°F to 176°F for heads and about 208°F for tails. This is where you get consistently pure alcohol carrying the actual aroma and flavor of grain.

What makes the hearts cut such a difficult call is the trade-off between cleanliness and complexity. A distiller who pulls a very tight, precise hearts cut produces a lighter, cleaner spirit, highly consistent batch to batch, but potentially one-dimensional. The problem is that unless the goal is textbook neutral spirit, that finished product can lack the range that makes barrel aging interesting. A bourbon that goes in thin tends to come out thin. On the other hand, running a wider cut pulls in more late-heads and early-tails, which introduces a broader range of congeners, esters, and fusel compounds that barrel aging transforms into the nutty, fruity, and savory notes serious collectors tend to prize. This is part of why big, high-proof expressions often reward a wider cut: there’s more raw material for the wood to work on over years of aging.

Distillers spend years dialing in their cut points, balancing alcohol yield against the flavor compounds they want in the hearts. At most serious operations, those cut points are treated as a house recipe.

What are the tails, and do any distillers actually use them?

The tails are the last portion of alcohol off the still. This fraction contains heavier alcohols, a much higher percentage of water, and other by-products that are more water-soluble. Like the heads, the tails carry compounds with higher boiling points than ethanol, and the result is often a harsh, bitter, oily character that’s obvious even in small concentrations.

But “discard everything” isn’t the only approach. Some distillers collect a portion of the tails separately, to be redistilled in subsequent runs or used for other purposes. Recycling tails back into the next charge is a traditional technique that adds body and a certain earthy richness. It’s a low-level contribution, but over many barrels and many years it can distinguish one producer’s house style from another’s. If you’ve noticed a subtle grain-forward earthiness in certain craft bourbons that separates them from squeaky-clean column-distilled expressions, tails recycling is often part of the explanation.

Most American bourbon producers run a hybrid system: a continuous column still for stripping, then a batch-style doubler where the practical cut decisions happen. The column still operates more or less continuously and doesn’t lend itself to traditional fraction collection the way a pot still does. The real art of the cut lives in that second vessel.

How do distillation cuts connect to the flavor notes I read in tasting notes?

Readers frequently ask: if cuts are invisible on the label, how do you detect their influence when you’re actually drinking?

Look for these signals in the glass. A bourbon with noticeable fruity or floral esters, think banana, pear, or dried apricot, often reflects a distillery that includes a bit of late-heads in their hearts run, since those volatile esters concentrate at the heads-hearts boundary. A savory, slightly meaty or nutty mid-palate, common in very old or very high-proof expressions, frequently owes something to fusel-oil contributions from a wider tails inclusion. A particularly clean, grain-pure new make, easy to assess on distillery tours when they offer white-dog samples, usually signals a tight, conservative cut.

None of this appears on the label. But when you’re building tasting notes or trying to understand why two expressions from the same distillery taste different, cut philosophy is one of the invisible variables worth considering alongside rickhouse position and age.

Does knowing about cuts help me evaluate bottles in my collection?

Yes, and it’s one of the reasons a detailed tasting journal pays off over time. Making the cut between heads, hearts, and tails is both a craft judgment and a technical process. Distillers rely on their senses as well as precise temperature and pressure readings to decide when to switch vessels, and those decisions carry real weight in the finished product. Once you internalize that, you start reading flavor patterns across your cellar differently.

For collectors who track multiple expressions from the same distillery across different years or proof points, noting whether a bottle leans fruity-and-floral versus earthy-and-oily is genuinely useful provenance information. If you’re using Pour Picks to log your collection, the free-text tasting notes field is the right place to capture these impressions, especially on distillery-exclusive picks or single barrels where house cut philosophy shows through more clearly than in a batched expression built for consistency. Over time, those notes become a personal map of which producers make the cuts that match your palate.

FAQs

What are the heads in bourbon distillation? The heads are the first fraction to come off the still. They contain volatile compounds like acetaldehyde and acetone, the source of nail-polish-remover aromas, and small amounts of methanol. Distillers discard most of the heads before collecting the usable spirit.

What is the hearts cut and why does it matter for flavor? The hearts are the clean, flavorful middle portion of the distillation run, the fraction that gets barreled and eventually bottled. A wider hearts cut means more congeners and complexity; a tighter cut produces a cleaner, lighter spirit. Where a distiller draws that line is one of the most closely guarded decisions in production.

What are the tails in bourbon and are they dangerous? The tails are the final portion of the run. They contain heavier fusel alcohols and little ethanol, giving them an oily, bitter quality. They’re not dangerous in normal quantities, but undesirable in large amounts. Some distillers recycle a small amount of tails back into the next run to add body and complexity.

How do distillation cuts affect the taste of bourbon? Cuts determine which chemical compounds reach the barrel. A distiller who runs a wider hearts cut pulls in more congeners, producing a more full-bodied, complex spirit after aging. A narrow, precise cut yields a lighter, cleaner bourbon. The same mashbill distilled with different cut points can taste noticeably different in the finished bottle.

Do bourbon distilleries distill once or twice? Most bourbon distilleries use a two-step process: a column still (beer still) strips alcohol from the fermented mash into a low wine, then a doubler or thumper makes a second pass to concentrate and refine the spirit. The practical heads-hearts-tails separation happens primarily in that second distillation stage.
