How Bourbon Is Made: A Collector's Guide to Every Step, From Grain to Glass

How bourbon is made, explained for collectors: mashing, fermentation, distillation, barrel entry, and aging — and why each step shapes what's in your bottle.

By Pour Picks · Published November 3, 2026

In brief

Bourbon is made by mashing a grain bill of at least 51% corn, fermenting it with yeast for three to five days, distilling the wash to no higher than 160 proof, then aging the new spirit in a brand-new charred oak barrel. The full process, grain to barrel, takes roughly one to two weeks; aging takes years.

Bourbon is one of the most regulated spirits in the world, and once you understand why those regulations exist, every bottle in your collection starts telling a richer story. Whether you’re trying to decode a label, decide which shelf to pull from, or explain to a friend why two bourbons from the same distillery can taste completely different, the production process is the answer hiding in plain sight.

What actually goes into making bourbon before the barrel even enters the picture?

A question we hear often: most collectors know aging matters, but the flavor journey starts much earlier, at the grain level.

Bourbon’s legal identity begins with its grain recipe, called the mash bill. Federal law requires that at least 51% of the grain be corn; the rest is typically rye, wheat, or malted barley in varying proportions. That corn minimum is why bourbon tends sweeter and fuller-bodied than Scotch or Irish whiskey. The secondary grain, often called the “flavor grain,” shapes the spirit’s personality before a drop touches wood. A high-rye bourbon delivers black pepper and dried fruit; a wheated bourbon leans soft, honey-sweet, and approachable.

Malted barley, though usually only 5-15% of the bill, plays an outsized enzymatic role: its natural enzymes break down the starches in corn and rye into simple sugars that yeast can ferment. Without it, you’d have a mash with nowhere to go. Corn goes in first at the highest temperature, followed by rye, and finally malted barley at a lower temperature to activate its enzymes for starch-to-sugar conversion. The order isn’t arbitrary. Each grain gelatinizes at a different temperature, and getting that sequence right is the first flavor decision a distillery makes every single day.

How does fermentation shape what ends up in my glass?

Fermentation is where alcohol comes into existence. Bourbon fermentation typically takes three to five days, resulting in a low-proof wash of around 8-10% alcohol, essentially a strong, unhopped beer. That liquid is called “distiller’s beer,” and it carries the earliest flavor compounds that will eventually define the finished whiskey.

Yeast strain is a surprisingly guarded secret at major distilleries. Some houses, Brown-Forman and Four Roses among the best-known examples, maintain proprietary yeast cultures passed down across generations, treating them with the same care as a sourdough starter. Fermentation temperature matters enormously too. Warmer fermentation (80-90°F) produces more fruity esters but risks off-flavors, while cooler fermentation (60-70°F) is slower but cleaner. Those esters, think banana, stone fruit, citrus peel, can survive distillation and aging in diluted but detectable form. A distillery that ferments warm and fast is making a different stylistic bet than one that runs cool and slow, even if their mash bills are identical.

Most large-scale bourbon production also uses a technique called sour mash: a portion of the spent mash liquid (called “backset” or “setback”) from a previous distillation run is blended into the new fermentation. This leftover liquid helps the new mash reach the correct pH levels. The practice came about in the 1800s as a way to produce a consistent product across multiple batches.

What happens during distillation, and why does proof matter so much?

This one comes up a lot: collectors often treat proof as a bottling decision, but it’s really a production decision that starts at the still.

Most bourbon is distilled in a two-step process. A continuous column still, sometimes called a beer still, concentrates the fermented wash from roughly 8-10% ABV up to around 55-70% ABV. That liquid, called “low wine,” then passes through a secondary still (a doubler or thumper) for a second distillation that refines and concentrates the spirit further. Federal law caps distillation at 160 proof (80% ABV); anything above that loses the legal right to be called bourbon. That ceiling isn’t arbitrary. Higher distillation proof strips away the grain-derived congeners that give bourbon its identity.

During this process, the master distiller manages what are called cut points, the precise moments when the flow of distillate is redirected from one collection vessel to another. Three main fractions come off the still: foreshots (heads), heart, and feints (tails). The foreshots contain harsh, potentially harmful compounds and are always discarded or recycled. The hearts, the clean, aromatic middle fraction, are what goes into the barrel. A wider heart cut allows more heavier aromatic compounds into the distillate, producing a richer character. A narrower cut produces something cleaner and lighter.

If you’ve read our article on heads, hearts, and tails, you already know the vocabulary. The cut point is where that knowledge meets the production floor, and it’s one of the least-talked-about ways two distilleries can produce dramatically different whiskeys from nearly identical mash bills.

After distillation, the new spirit is diluted with water to barrel entry proof, which must be 125 proof or lower by law. Many craft distilleries enter significantly lower, sometimes at 100-110 proof, because lower entry proof leaves more water in the barrel, which creates a different texture and extraction rate over time.

Industry estimates suggest that 60-80% of a mature bourbon’s flavor comes directly from its interaction with the barrel, making the wood arguably the most important ingredient in the bottle. After four years in a barrel, bourbon typically contains 3-5 mg/L of vanillin, the compound responsible for that signature vanilla character every bourbon drinker recognizes.

The barrel must be new, charred American white oak. That charring requirement, legally mandatory, does two things simultaneously: the carbon layer acts as a natural filter for harsh sulfur compounds in the new spirit, and the heat cracks open the wood’s hemicellulose, creating sugars that caramelize into the familiar caramel and toffee notes. The deeper the char level, the more aggressive that filtration and caramelization effect. A Number 4 “alligator char” looks almost burnt; a Number 1 char is barely more than toasted. Both are legal. Both produce very different whiskeys.

How long does it actually take to make bourbon from grain to bottle?

Readers frequently ask: whether the years on the label account for the whole process, or just the barrel time.

The grain-to-barrel steps, mashing, fermentation, distillation, and barrel entry, can be completed in roughly one to two weeks. The years collectors talk about, the 8-year, the 12-year, the 23-year, refer entirely to the time the spirit spends in wood. There is no minimum aging requirement for plain bourbon, but to carry a straight bourbon designation the whiskey must spend at least two years in the barrel, and any bourbon aged fewer than four years must display its age on the label.

Most age at least a year or two, and many experts agree bourbon becomes mellower and more drinkable with time, often peaking somewhere around five to ten years. Climate accelerates the process. The dramatic temperature swings in a Kentucky rickhouse force the spirit in and out of the wood’s pores far more aggressively than the temperate climates of Scotland, which is part of why Kentucky bourbon matures faster than Scotch at comparable ages.

Kentucky is currently holding a record 16.1 million barrels of aging bourbon, according to the Kentucky Distillers’ Association, a number that reflects decades of investment in production capacity responding to the global bourbon boom.

Once it’s aged, what happens before it reaches the shelf?

After the distillery decides a barrel (or a set of barrels) is ready, the whiskey is dumped and either bottled as a single barrel, blended with other barrels into a small batch, or married together for a period before bottling. Bourbon must be bottled at a minimum of 80 proof (40% ABV). Some distillers blend various barrels together and then add water. Others bottle at a higher alcohol level because it can be more flavorful. Still others add no water at all, referred to as cask strength.

Most bourbons are also chill-filtered to prevent the natural fatty acids and proteins in the spirit from causing haze when chilled or water is added. Non-chill filtered bourbons skip that step, which many collectors prefer because it preserves more of the texture and mouthfeel compounds the barrel worked hard to develop.

Does where a bourbon is made actually change how it tastes?

Beyond Kentucky versus everywhere else, regional production conditions create real and measurable differences. Kentucky’s limestone geology filters iron from the water supply while adding calcium and magnesium, conditions that support healthy yeast fermentation and have made this region the center of American bourbon production for more than two centuries. Iron in water is unfriendly to yeast and creates off-notes in the finished spirit; limestone filtration naturally removes it.

Texas bourbons age faster due to extreme summer heat (barrels in a Texas warehouse can experience 130°F+ temperatures), producing whiskeys with aggressive wood character at younger ages. Colorado distilleries at altitude work with lower atmospheric pressure, which affects distillation dynamics. These aren’t marketing stories. They’re chemistry. Whether bourbon has terroir in the strict sense is debated, but production environment undeniably shapes what ends up in the glass.

Understanding the full production arc, from the grain bill your distillery chose, through the fermentation style and cut points, to the specific barrel and the floor of the rickhouse it rested on, is what separates a collector who owns bottles from one who genuinely knows them. Every label is shorthand for a thousand decisions made over years. The more fluently you can read those decisions, the better your cellar gets.

Frequently Asked Questions

What grains are used to make bourbon?

Bourbon must contain at least 51% corn. The remaining grains, typically rye, wheat, or malted barley, make up the mash bill. High-rye bourbons are spicier; wheated bourbons are softer and sweeter. Malted barley, usually 5, 15% of the bill, provides the enzymes needed to convert starch into fermentable sugar.

How long does bourbon fermentation take?

Bourbon fermentation typically runs three to five days at commercial distilleries, producing a low-proof wash of roughly 8, 10% ABV. Longer fermentation windows, up to seven days at some craft distilleries, can develop more fruity ester compounds that carry through to the finished whiskey.

What proof must bourbon be distilled and barreled at?

Federal law requires bourbon to be distilled at no higher than 160 proof (80% ABV) and entered into the barrel at no higher than 125 proof (62.5% ABV). These caps preserve grain-forward flavor; higher-proof distillation strips out character. Many premium bourbons are barreled well below the legal maximum.

Does bourbon have to be made in Kentucky?

No. Bourbon can legally be made anywhere in the United States. However, to be labeled 'Kentucky Straight Bourbon,' it must be distilled and aged for at least one year in Kentucky. The state produces the vast majority of the world's bourbon supply.

How does the barrel make bourbon taste the way it does?

New charred oak barrels are the single biggest driver of bourbon flavor. The char acts as a carbon filter while also cracking open the wood's sugars. Seasonal temperature swings force the liquid in and out of the wood's pores, extracting vanillin, caramel compounds, tannins, and spice. Estimates suggest 60, 80% of a mature bourbon's flavor comes from the barrel interaction.
